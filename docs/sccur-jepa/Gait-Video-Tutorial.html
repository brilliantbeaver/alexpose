Student guide

How to study walking with video

Imagine a camera records one walk from the side and another from the front. Could a computer recognize the same walking pattern in both? What if it learns that one camera angle tends to appear with one gait label? This tutorial follows the researchers as they design experiments that can tell those possibilities apart. You only need averages, fractions, and a little curiosity about how people move.

The short answer. A model that knew a walker's current posture predicted their next few leg positions more accurately than a rule that kept every joint still. Recognizing an unusual gait was harder. In a separate test, a classifier trained to read S-JEPA features earned the same balanced accuracy as one trained to read features from an encoder with random weights. The lessons explain how to check both claims yourself.

Start with one recording

Gait is the pattern a person makes while walking. A landmark is a point the computer estimates on the body, such as a knee. A pose is the collection of those points in one frame. A landmark can disappear behind the other leg. The experiment keeps track of which coordinates were actually observed.

A recording is one source video. A sequence is a labeled stretch of walking in that video. Researchers sample short windows from a sequence so a model can work with a fixed number of observations. If you watch three windows from one sequence, you have three views of one walk. Counting them as three different people would give a misleading impression of how much independent evidence you have.

Notebooks 00–07

What can a computer learn from watching someone walk?

Your gait is the way you walk. Imagine filming the same walker twice, once from the side and once from the front. Their gait might stay the same while the visible pattern of knees and ankles changes dramatically. Now imagine cutting a single film into ten clips. A computer that recognizes all ten may have learned something about walking, the viewpoint, the person, or the recording. How could you tell which?

This guide follows notebooks 00–07 in the original AlexPose worldmodels experiment. They teach a method called Skeleton Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (S-JEPA). The computer tracks body points such as knees and ankles, called landmarks. During training, part of the model sees some of those points hidden. It predicts features, numbers that describe what was hidden. Another part sees the whole clip and supplies the numbers to aim for. Researchers call these two parts the student and the teacher. Later, researchers test what those features can do. Three different questions require three different answers.

Question What information is available? What counts as success?
Can we fill in missing information? Some observed joints are deliberately hidden. Other times in the same clip can be visible. The student predicts the teacher's numerical description of the hidden observations.
Can we predict the future? Only the beginning of the clip is visible to the predictor. Predicted joint positions are close to later observations, compared with simply repeating the last position.
Can we recognize a gait label in a new video? A classifier is a model that assigns a label. Here it reads a short set of video frames, called a window. Its label for a new recording agrees with the label supplied by the dataset.

Which results belong here? These eight notebooks use 16 selected landmarks. The released classifier comparison has 405 windows from 89 recordings. The longer forecasting experiment has 407 windows from 91 recordings. Each retained annotated walking sequence supplies one short window in each experiment for which it qualifies. The poster also discusses a separate 33-landmark GAVD3 study with 96 clips, and a later forecasting benchmark uses 456 sequences and multiple windows per sequence. Those are different experiments. In particular, the later benchmark's 40.8% error reduction belongs to a different model that learns to predict future positions from the current posture. Original study and cohorts · Later benchmark

Follow the eight notebooks

00 · Decide what counts as one example

Start with a source (an original video), a sequence (an annotated stretch of walking within it), and a window (the sampled frames used as model input). The notebook rebuilds the Gait Abnormality Video Dataset inventory, records unavailable or excluded observations, assigns whole source videos to separate groups, and saves a record of those choices. If one source contains six walking sequences, all six go to the same partition. Otherwise a test clip can resemble a clip the model already saw.

The model learns from the training recordings. Researchers use a separate validation group to choose settings and a test group to measure how well the chosen model performs on new footage. Different uploads can still show the same person, so an unseen source is not necessarily an unseen participant. The labels here are video annotations for normal, Parkinson's, stroke, cerebral palsy and myopathic gait. They are the targets for the study, not new diagnoses of the people in the videos. Notebook 00

01 · Check that the skeleton follows the right person

The notebook locates an annotated video interval, maps its frame numbers to the decoder's numbering, extracts a short pose sequence and inspects it. GAVD calls its first frame 1. The decoder, the software that reads the video, calls that same frame 0. Converting annotation frame 101 to decoder frame 100 keeps the pose matched to the right image. A frame can contain a patient, an assistant and a camera cut. A confident pose on the wrong person can look smooth, so visual review matters. Two entire abnormal sources were ultimately excluded after reviewers found tracking or scene problems.

BlazePose estimates landmarks in each image. Here the coordinates describe 2D motion relative to the hips. Subtract the pelvis midpoint, then divide by the median observed torso length from the first eight context frames. An ankle one torso length below the hips is roughly comparable across large and small images. This cannot tell you how many metres a person walked. The pose detector assigns each position a confidence score. The study counts a position as observed only when its confidence is at least 0.5. The default quality check also asks whether at least 70% of the positions were observed. It keeps a separate record of which landmarks were observed. A missing coordinate filled with zero for computation is still missing. Notebook 01 · Joint and measurement guide

02 · Make a prediction puzzle the model cannot solve by peeking

The study keeps the left and right shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, heels and forefeet. A token is one joint at one sampled time. A 16-frame window has 16 × 16 = 256 potential tokens. If all are observed, hiding half randomly leaves 128 observed tokens as targets and 128 as visible clues. In real videos only valid tokens enter this draw. If the ankle and wrist are both visible, each has the same chance of being hidden, even if the ankle is moving much faster.

Compare two puzzles. Hide the middle ankle in a three-frame clip and the model can use the frames on either side. Hide the whole future and it must predict from earlier observations alone. The notebook builds both masks and tests that changing motion values cannot change the uniform-mask selection. The artificial mask is applied to normalized coordinates; hip and shoulder measurements may already have contributed to normalization. Notebook 02 · Masking implementation

03 · Learn useful features from normal walking

An encoder converts positions into a list of numbers called a feature vector. The student encoder sees visible joint tokens. A predictor uses them to estimate a hidden token's features. A teacher encoder sees the observed clip and supplies a training target. Its adjustable numbers, called weights, follow a slowly changing average of the student encoder’s weights. The target therefore changes slowly as learning proceeds. Normal labels select the clips for this stage, while the clip supplies the prediction target.

There is a tempting shortcut: if both encoders always output the same constant vector, matching them would be easy. The notebook explores this collapse with toy vectors and checks real training diagnostics. The variance term encourages features to vary across examples. The covariance term discourages many dimensions from copying the same signal. These are two ingredients of VICReg, a method for regularizing representations. Varying features are a starting point; a classifier and a forecast test still have to show what information they carry. Notebook 03 · Model implementation

04 · Compare ways to introduce more kinds of walking

Make a copy of the normal-trained encoder. In staged adaptation, introduce condition groups in sequence and retain examples from earlier groups. In mixed adaptation, use all the training groups together. Match the total number of training updates so extra practice alone cannot explain a difference. Keep the starting normal model intact for comparison. Labels organize the training groups here; predicting the hidden observation still supplies the learning task.

A different feature vector after adaptation only tells you that the representation changed. Notebook 06 asks whether those changes help a separately fitted classifier. Before comparing test results, the researchers chose the mixed model for their main comparison. They reported the staged model as an additional comparison. Notebook 04

05 · Forecast coordinates and test the forecast against a simple rule

Persistence means repeating the latest observed position. A direct predictor estimates future coordinates; a residual predictor starts at the latest pose and estimates a change. The notebook illustrates a short past/future split. The released forecasting experiment uses a longer window of 16 past and 16 future frames, sampling every second native video frame. Across test sources the future spans roughly 1.07–1.33 seconds. Frame counts alone do not specify elapsed time.

Try one coordinate. These are teaching numbers. Suppose the ankle's last horizontal position was 0.20 torso lengths and its next observed position is 0.30. Persistence predicts 0.20, for squared error (0.30 − 0.20)² = 0.0100. A model predicting 0.28 has squared error 0.0004. That is a good prediction for this one coordinate. Average errors across all supported targets and compare on multiple held-out recordings before making a claim. In the released study, the primary residual model's mean advantage was only about 0.0005 on six normal test sources, and the uncertainty range included zero. Some plausible results favor repeating the last position. The model had also missed the researchers’ advance target on validation recordings.

A stochastic model can generate several possible futures from the same past. In a different test, researchers slightly varied the time positions the model was given during training. Such timing changes do not generate multiple possible futures by themselves. The experiment compared 16 sampled futures with one deterministic forecast using an energy score, which evaluates how close the samples are to the observation while accounting for their spread. On average the sampled model lowered the energy score by 0.00727. Lower scores are better on this measure. The sampled model had additional parameters, and its spread was a poorer guide to when predictions would fail than simple input-quality information. Notebook 05 · Released forecast results and controls

06 · Test the classifier on new recordings

Here the inputs are 16-frame windows. One approach computes 37 predefined measurements, including projected joint angles, their ranges, and side-to-side differences. A random forest combines votes from many decision trees built from those measurements. The other approach freezes an encoder, meaning its weights stop changing. Researchers train a simple classifier, called a linear probe, to combine the encoder’s features into a prediction. The probe is trained even when the encoder is randomly initialized. This gives a useful comparison. How well can the classifier work when the encoder has never learned from a walking video?

The classifier study used 53 training, 19 validation and 17 test source recordings. Six test recordings had a normal label and 11 had an abnormal label. Test windows receive probabilities, which are averaged within sequence and then within recording. A held-out walking interval goes through the same pose preparation as training. When making a prediction on a new recording, the encoder receives available measurements from the 16 selected joints without deliberate training masks. Classification reads an observed interval; predicting its future is a separate task.

Classifier for normal versus abnormal gait Balanced accuracy on 17 test recordings
Random forest on 37 gait measurements 78.8%
Linear probe on a randomly initialized encoder 81.8%
Probe after normal-only pretraining 78.0%
Probe after mixed adaptation (primary) 81.8%
Probe after staged adaptation (secondary) 90.9%

Reproduce one score by hand. The mixed model correctly classified 6 of the 6 normal recordings and 7 of the 11 abnormal recordings. Balanced accuracy gives each class equal weight: (6/6 + 7/11) ÷ 2 ≈ 81.8%. Ordinary accuracy would be 13/17 ≈ 76.5%. The random forest correctly classified 4/6 normal and 10/11 abnormal recordings. Both error patterns matter. The mixed model's balanced accuracy exceeded the forest's by 3.0 percentage points, with a paired 95% interval from −18.9 to +25.0 points. The control using random encoder features reached the same balanced accuracy as the mixed model. These observations leave the advantage of pretraining unresolved. Five-label recognition and a direct normal/abnormal classifier are different tasks, with different training targets. Notebook 06 · Source-level evaluation · Released scores

07 · Check which explanations survive the controls

An ablation changes one part of a method and measures the effect. The final notebook traces model settings, real versus example data, validation decisions, saved predictions and release records. It asks how much a claim depends on the prediction objective, the order of earlier frames, the fitted representation or the way uncertainty is scored. It retains unfavorable comparisons as well as favorable ones.

For instance, shuffling the first fifteen frames of the past while retaining its endpoint barely changed the primary forecast error. The result gives little support here to the idea that this predictor needed detailed timing. A separate direct predictor was a relatively poor normal forecaster, yet one secondary score ranked abnormal recordings well in this sample. Ranking asks whether a recording with an abnormal label tends to receive a higher score, whereas forecast accuracy asks how close a predicted future was to the actual joint positions. A small, repeatable movement can be easy to predict and still carry an abnormal label.

A bootstrap interval repeatedly resamples whole recordings to see how much a comparison changes. Resampling individual frames would treat highly related observations as independent. A seed sets the starting point for random choices in a training run. Trying several seeds shows whether changing those choices affects the results. Each run still uses the same people. This experiment used one seed for the classifier release and three for the forecasting comparison. The test recordings came from the study's existing data collection. Wider clinical generalization would require independent participants, acquisition settings and validation. Notebook 07 · Release protocol · Independent scientific review

Does the timing of the other leg help predict a step?

Pause a side-view video of someone walking. Their right ankle is behind their hip. Would knowing where the left ankle was over the past second improve your guess about where the right ankle will be next? If it does, how could you tell whether the timing of those earlier positions mattered, or whether the last picture already told you most of what you needed?

This is the question behind notebooks 10–16. The earlier S-JEPA experiment learned numerical representations of masked skeleton observations. The restart deliberately made the prediction task easier to inspect. It fitted small models that predict future joint coordinates, compared their predictions with simple alternatives, and challenged the interpretation of their errors. The distinction matters whenever you describe the results. An error reduction for a model using both legs is evidence about that coordinate predictor. 11 · Find the right person, frame and time

The notebook selects the first eligible example in a fixed sort order, decodes a real video frame and calls the shared Ambient pose estimator. The source annotation numbers its first frame 1. OpenCV numbers it 0. For illustration, annotation frame 25 corresponds to decoder index 24. In an ideal constant-rate 24-frames-per-second clip starting at zero, that frame occurs at 1.000 seconds. Accidentally asking for index 25 would advance the example by another 1/24 second.

A timestamp and a frame count answer different questions. The researchers requested a grid with one observation every 0.1 seconds, selected the nearest actual frames and retained the decoded times. In the same illustrative clip, a request at 1.100 seconds falls between native frames at 1.0833 and 1.1250 seconds. The nearer is 1.0833 seconds; a stored coordinate at that frame is an observation. Inventing a landmark halfway between frames would create a different kind of measurement. In the study, the elapsed time from the final input to the final future observation ranged from 0.767 to 0.834 seconds around the nominal 0.8-second horizon.

Inspect the overlaid person and landmarks and the decisions recorded in the pose cache. A detected pelvis inside a walking person’s box can still belong to somebody else if two people overlap. The study’s person checks and confidence gates reduce this risk; they cannot identify every identity switch. Notebook 11 · Measurements and actual horizon

12 · Give the model a past without leaking the future

A window has 16 past samples and eight future samples. The target joints are the knees, ankles, heels and front-of-foot landmarks on both sides. Subtracting the pelvis midpoint expresses each landmark relative to the body. Dividing by a torso length estimated from the past gives the model comparable units for nearer and farther people. It still sees a 2D projection. Even an “own-leg” predictor shares some information from both sides because both hips define the pelvis midpoint.

The notebook displays a grid of valid observations, then deliberately changes future coordinates and future visibility flags. The features fed to the predictor must stay identical. A false visibility flag means the estimator did not produce a usable observation. A stored zero at that location does not mean that the ankle sat at the pelvis. An error can be calculated only when a future target has the required observed support.

Consider the control that disrupts the timing of the opposite leg. Applying the same permutation everywhere would just rename the columns; a regression could learn the new names. The corrected control shifts the historical opposite-leg samples by a window-specific offset, moves their validity flags along with them, and preserves the latest anchor. It gives a second model the same earlier values with their timing misaligned. Notebook 12 · Control-design correction

13 · Fit on normal walking and test on different recordings

First the notebook fits a teaching model on a small batch of real normal observations. The model learns displacement from the last observed position. Ridge regression penalizes large coefficients so one noisy input is less likely to dominate. If the learned displacement is zero, the prediction stays at the last position. This simple forecast is called persistence. In the optional shrinkage comparison, a multiplier between zero and one pulls the learned displacement toward persistence.

The demonstration’s error is computed on material used for fitting. For the actual experiment, every source was assigned to one of five outer folds. One outer fold at a time was set aside. Within the remaining normal training sources, three inner folds selected regularization strength; the optional shrinkage model also selected its multiplier there. A recording’s abnormal annotation or outer test error could not select these settings. The full run saved the assignments, decisions and input identities. Notebook 13 · Selected settings by fold and leg

Try it. A fitted model proposes a displacement of +0.40 torso lengths from an anchor of 0.10. What does a multiplier of 0.5 predict? The answer is 0.10 + 0.5 × 0.40 = 0.30. A multiplier of zero predicts 0.10. Trying all multipliers on the final test recording and reporting the best would leak that recording’s future into model selection.

15 · Decide which sentence could go in a paper

The notebook compares the protocol, the movement-science rationale and the completed artifacts. Try defending the sentence “The model measured neurological coordination.” Where is the measurement of nerves, muscle activity or verified patient status? Those data are absent. A sentence the experiment can support is “A model trained on both legs’ past positions predicted observed future positions better than repeating the last pose.” Its comparator and its measurement are explicit.

16 · Recalculate the anomaly comparison and design a fair mask

The excess-error score subtracts persistence error from the bilateral model’s error. Larger values were designated more abnormal before the restart evaluation. On the 91 source recordings, raw bilateral error had pooled AUROC 0.304, while excess error reached 0.793. AUROC measures how often an abnormal-annotated recording ranks above a normal one, giving half credit for ties. Later, after seeing the study results, the team audited a simpler rule: score recordings higher when the observed future moves less. This post hoc low-movement score reached 0.806. The paired difference, excess minus low movement, was −0.013, with a 95% interval [−0.111, 0.083]. There is an association with the annotations, but no demonstrated extra ranking benefit over the simple rule. Both scores require the observed future. Notebook 16 · Anomaly table · Score audit

The second exercise designs an experiment. A joint hidden behind another person is naturally missing. A visible joint deliberately withheld from the model is masked. The laboratory compares four ways to choose past observations to hide: uniform random, adjacent time blocks, connected limb groups, and a distribution across motion levels. It protects the reference joints and latest observed anchors. For one real example, there were 174 eligible tokens and a 40% budget. Since 0.40 × 174 = 69.6, every policy hid 70. The laboratory checked all four policies on one representative window from each of 91 sources. The 364 passing checks establish that the implemented masking rules satisfied those checks. This notebook did not fit competing masked models. Mask laboratory validation

A pose estimator places landmarks on the body in each frame. 21 · Build a reference for what counts as surprising

First try persistence. Predict that each joint will stay at its latest observed position. If the leg barely moves, this forecast can do well even when the walk is visibly restricted. This is why the study also scores low movement. It assigns a higher anomaly score when the eventual positions stay closer to their starting points. It needs the future video to calculate this score.

The more complex score asks whether a recording was unusually easy or unusually hard to forecast relative to normal walking with similar past motion and missingness. The researchers fit the forecaster using normal recordings, estimated the expected error from motion and missingness using predictions for examples held out of the respective fits, then calibrated the residual error against a separate group of normal recordings. An outer test recording contributed to none of these fits. Calibration used only seven or eight normal recordings at a time. Averaging three rotations reduces dependence on one assignment but still gives a coarse reference. The resulting scores can rank recordings; a clinical alarm threshold would need additional evidence. 23 · Fit the simplest model that can learn motion

Persistence cannot learn. A ridge regression can learn that a particular posture tends to precede a particular displacement, while penalizing very large coefficients. It predicts that displacement relative to the latest observed positions. These coefficients are the weights the model gives each input when combining positions into a prediction. Other models received one leg’s history, both legs’ histories, or current posture plus recent velocity and reliability information. A small neural network predicted future coordinates in the masking comparison. It had a different objective from predicting teacher embeddings in S-JEPA.

The notebook fits a small teaching model on real normal examples. Its training error merely checks that the fitting procedure runs. For the study results, five outer folds held out whole recording groups, and three inner normal-recording folds chose model settings. Settings were chosen before using the outer test results. A useful contamination check changes every future coordinate and its availability while leaving the past untouched. Predictions must stay the same. After the future arrives, availability determines which predicted coordinates can be graded. Notebook 23 · Frozen study design

24 · Grade the forecasts, then grade the rankings

Worked example. Suppose the last observed ankle x-coordinate is 0.20 torso lengths, the future measurement is 0.50 and the learned model predicts 0.40. On the 29 held-out normal recording groups, persistence had mean squared error (MSE) 0.12126 and current posture had MSE 0.07174. Each normal recording was tested in its own held-out fold. Now ask a different question. Draw one abnormal-annotated recording and one normal-annotated recording from the same test fold. How often does the abnormal one receive the higher anomaly score? AUROC measures this probability, with half credit for ties and 0.5 indicating chance ranking. The researchers combined folds according to their number of eligible pairs. On GAVD the conditional forecast score reached 0.462, versus 0.811 for low observed future movement. If a camera records a person from the front instead of the side, projected ankle displacement can change even if the walk is identical. The notebook explores recording view, lost landmarks, coordinate noise, timing shifts and external acquisition differences. These tests modify observations; they cannot simulate muscle activation or disease progression. Check your reasoning

  1. A friend randomly splits the 2,815 windows and calls the test independent. What should change? Assign the original recording groups first and put all related sequences and windows in their recording’s fold. A window split can repeat nearly identical steps across training and test.
  2. If the future ankle is obscured, should a predictor omit its output? No. Make a prediction for that ankle, but leave it out of the error calculation if it cannot be observed. A query is a request for one joint at a specified future time. During inference, the trained model answers each query using only the past. Letting future visibility change the inputs would leak information.
  3. Why test persistence if it is easy to beat on average? It establishes how much error comes from movement since the last measurement. Restricted movement can yield very low persistence error and a strong low-movement ranking score.
  4. Can we call 41.6% a second replication of the 40.8% result? Both estimates reuse identical saved predictions. The second answers a worthwhile sensitivity question about how to weight long versus short recordings.
  5. Why does 0.591 versus 0.762 not settle the external comparison? The paired participant-bootstrap interval includes zero. Those observed values favor low movement, but the data leave meaningful uncertainty.
