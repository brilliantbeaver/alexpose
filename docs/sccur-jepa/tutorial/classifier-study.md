# What can a computer learn from watching someone walk?

Your **gait** is the way you walk. Imagine filming the same walker twice, once from the side and once from the front. Their gait might stay the same while the visible pattern of knees and ankles changes dramatically. Now imagine cutting a single film into ten clips. A computer that recognizes all ten may have learned something about walking, the viewpoint, the person, or the recording. How could you tell which?

This guide follows notebooks **00–07** in the original AlexPose worldmodels experiment. They teach a method called **Skeleton Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (S-JEPA)**. The computer tracks body points such as knees and ankles, called **landmarks**. During training, part of the model sees some of those points hidden. It predicts **features**, numbers that describe what was hidden. Another part sees the whole clip and supplies the numbers to aim for. Researchers call these two parts the student and the teacher. Later, researchers test what those features can do. Three different questions require three different answers.

| Question | What information is available? | What counts as success? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can we fill in missing information? | Some observed joints are deliberately hidden. Other times in the same clip can be visible. | The student predicts the teacher's numerical description of the hidden observations. |
| Can we predict the future? | Only the beginning of the clip is visible to the predictor. | Predicted joint positions are close to later observations, compared with simply repeating the last position. |
| Can we recognize a gait label in a new video? | A **classifier** is a model that assigns a label. Here it reads a short set of video frames, called a **window**. | Its label for a new recording agrees with the label supplied by the dataset. |

**Which results belong here?** These eight notebooks use 16 selected landmarks. The released classifier comparison has **405 windows from 89 recordings**. The longer forecasting experiment has **407 windows from 91 recordings**. Each retained annotated walking sequence supplies one short window in each experiment for which it qualifies. The poster also discusses a separate 33-landmark GAVD3 study with 96 clips, and a later forecasting benchmark uses 456 sequences and multiple windows per sequence. Those are different experiments. In particular, the later benchmark's 40.8% error reduction belongs to a different model that learns to predict future positions from the current posture. ## Follow the eight notebooks

### 00 · Decide what counts as one example

Start with a **source** (an original video), a **sequence** (an annotated stretch of walking within it), and a **window** (the sampled frames used as model input). The notebook rebuilds the Gait Abnormality Video Dataset inventory, records unavailable or excluded observations, assigns whole source videos to separate groups, and saves a record of those choices. If one source contains six walking sequences, all six go to the same partition. Otherwise a test clip can resemble a clip the model already saw.

The model learns from the **training** recordings. Researchers use a separate **validation** group to choose settings and a **test** group to measure how well the chosen model performs on new footage. Different uploads can still show the same person, so an unseen source is not necessarily an unseen participant. The labels here are video annotations for normal, Parkinson's, stroke, cerebral palsy and myopathic gait. They are the targets for the study, not new diagnoses of the people in the videos. [Notebook 00](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/00_inventory_and_research_question.ipynb)

### 01 · Check that the skeleton follows the right person

The notebook locates an annotated video interval, maps its frame numbers to the decoder's numbering, extracts a short pose sequence and inspects it. GAVD calls its first frame 1. The decoder, the software that reads the video, calls that same frame 0. Converting annotation frame 101 to decoder frame 100 keeps the pose matched to the right image. A frame can contain a patient, an assistant and a camera cut. A confident pose on the wrong person can look smooth, so visual review matters. Two entire abnormal sources were ultimately excluded after reviewers found tracking or scene problems.

BlazePose estimates landmarks in each image. Here the coordinates describe **2D motion relative to the hips**. Subtract the pelvis midpoint, then divide by the median observed torso length from the first eight context frames. An ankle one torso length below the hips is roughly comparable across large and small images. This cannot tell you how many metres a person walked. The pose detector assigns each position a confidence score. The study counts a position as observed only when its confidence is at least 0.5. The default quality check also asks whether at least 70% of the positions were observed. It keeps a separate record of which landmarks were observed. A missing coordinate filled with zero for computation is still missing. [Notebook 01](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/01_video_pose_and_quality.ipynb) · [Joint and measurement guide](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/joints.md)

### 02 · Make a prediction puzzle the model cannot solve by peeking

The study keeps the left and right shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, heels and forefeet. A **token** is one joint at one sampled time. A 16-frame window has 16 × 16 = 256 potential tokens. If all are observed, hiding half randomly leaves 128 observed tokens as targets and 128 as visible clues. In real videos only valid tokens enter this draw. If the ankle and wrist are both visible, each has the same chance of being hidden, even if the ankle is moving much faster.

Compare two puzzles. Hide the middle ankle in a three-frame clip and the model can use the frames on either side. Hide the whole future and it must predict from earlier observations alone. The notebook builds both masks and tests that changing motion values cannot change the uniform-mask selection. The artificial mask is applied to normalized coordinates; hip and shoulder measurements may already have contributed to normalization. [Notebook 02](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/02_joint_tokens_and_uniform_masks.ipynb) · [Masking implementation](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/worldmodels/masking.py)

### 03 · Learn useful features from normal walking

An **encoder** converts positions into a list of numbers called a feature vector. The student encoder sees visible joint tokens. A predictor uses them to estimate a hidden token's features. A teacher encoder sees the observed clip and supplies a training target. Its adjustable numbers, called **weights**, follow a slowly changing average of the student encoder’s weights. The target therefore changes slowly as learning proceeds. Normal labels select the clips for this stage, while the clip supplies the prediction target.

There is a tempting shortcut: if both encoders always output the same constant vector, matching them would be easy. The notebook explores this **collapse** with toy vectors and checks real training diagnostics. The variance term encourages features to vary across examples. The covariance term discourages many dimensions from copying the same signal. These are two ingredients of VICReg, a method for regularizing representations. Varying features are a starting point; a classifier and a forecast test still have to show what information they carry. [Notebook 03](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/03_normal_only_jepa_and_vicreg.ipynb) · [Model implementation](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/worldmodels/model.py)

### 04 · Compare ways to introduce more kinds of walking

Make a copy of the normal-trained encoder. In **staged adaptation**, introduce condition groups in sequence and retain examples from earlier groups. In **mixed adaptation**, use all the training groups together. Match the total number of training updates so extra practice alone cannot explain a difference. Keep the starting normal model intact for comparison. Labels organize the training groups here; predicting the hidden observation still supplies the learning task.

A different feature vector after adaptation only tells you that the representation changed. Notebook 06 asks whether those changes help a separately fitted classifier. Before comparing test results, the researchers chose the mixed model for their main comparison. They reported the staged model as an additional comparison. [Notebook 04](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/04_progressive_condition_adaptation.ipynb)

### 05 · Forecast coordinates and test the forecast against a simple rule

**Persistence** means repeating the latest observed position. A **direct** predictor estimates future coordinates; a **residual** predictor starts at the latest pose and estimates a change. The notebook illustrates a short past/future split. The released forecasting experiment uses a longer window of 16 past and 16 future frames, sampling every second native video frame. Across test sources the future spans roughly 1.07–1.33 seconds. Frame counts alone do not specify elapsed time.

**Try one coordinate.** These are teaching numbers. Suppose the ankle's last horizontal position was 0.20 torso lengths and its next observed position is 0.30. Persistence predicts 0.20, for squared error (0.30 − 0.20)² = 0.0100. A model predicting 0.28 has squared error 0.0004. That is a good prediction for this one coordinate. Average errors across all supported targets and compare on multiple held-out recordings before making a claim. In the released study, the primary residual model's mean advantage was only about 0.0005 on six normal test sources, and the uncertainty range included zero. Some plausible results favor repeating the last position. The model had also missed the researchers’ advance target on validation recordings.

A **stochastic** model can generate several possible futures from the same past. In a different test, researchers slightly varied the time positions the model was given during training. Such timing changes do not generate multiple possible futures by themselves. The experiment compared 16 sampled futures with one deterministic forecast using an **energy score**, which evaluates how close the samples are to the observation while accounting for their spread. On average the sampled model lowered the energy score by 0.00727. Lower scores are better on this measure. The sampled model had additional parameters, and its spread was a poorer guide to when predictions would fail than simple input-quality information. [Notebook 05](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/05_forecasting_and_stochasticity.ipynb) · [Released forecast results and controls](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/final_scientific_review.md)

### 06 · Test the classifier on new recordings

Here the inputs are 16-frame windows. One approach computes **37 predefined measurements**, including projected joint angles, their ranges, and side-to-side differences. A **random forest** combines votes from many decision trees built from those measurements. The other approach **freezes** an encoder, meaning its weights stop changing. Researchers train a simple classifier, called a **linear probe**, to combine the encoder’s features into a prediction. The probe is trained even when the encoder is randomly initialized. This gives a useful comparison. How well can the classifier work when the encoder has never learned from a walking video?

The classifier study used 53 training, 19 validation and 17 test source recordings. Six test recordings had a normal label and 11 had an abnormal label. Test windows receive probabilities, which are averaged within sequence and then within recording. A held-out walking interval goes through the same pose preparation as training. When making a prediction on a new recording, the encoder receives available measurements from the 16 selected joints without deliberate training masks. Classification reads an observed interval; predicting its future is a separate task.

| Classifier for normal versus abnormal gait | Balanced accuracy on 17 test recordings |
| --- | ---: |
| Random forest on 37 gait measurements | 78.8% |
| Linear probe on a randomly initialized encoder | 81.8% |
| Probe after normal-only pretraining | 78.0% |
| Probe after mixed adaptation (primary) | 81.8% |
| Probe after staged adaptation (secondary) | 90.9% |

**Reproduce one score by hand.** The mixed model correctly classified 6 of the 6 normal recordings and 7 of the 11 abnormal recordings. Balanced accuracy gives each class equal weight: (6/6 + 7/11) ÷ 2 ≈ 81.8%. Ordinary accuracy would be 13/17 ≈ 76.5%. The random forest correctly classified 4/6 normal and 10/11 abnormal recordings. Both error patterns matter. The mixed model's balanced accuracy exceeded the forest's by 3.0 percentage points, with a paired 95% interval from −18.9 to +25.0 points. The control using random encoder features reached the same balanced accuracy as the mixed model. These observations leave the advantage of pretraining unresolved. Five-label recognition and a direct normal/abnormal classifier are different tasks, with different training targets. ### 07 · Check which explanations survive the controls

An **ablation** changes one part of a method and measures the effect. The final notebook traces model settings, real versus example data, validation decisions, saved predictions and release records. It asks how much a claim depends on the prediction objective, the order of earlier frames, the fitted representation or the way uncertainty is scored. It retains unfavorable comparisons as well as favorable ones.

For instance, shuffling the first fifteen frames of the past while retaining its endpoint barely changed the primary forecast error. The result gives little support here to the idea that this predictor needed detailed timing. A separate direct predictor was a relatively poor normal forecaster, yet one secondary score ranked abnormal recordings well in this sample. **Ranking** asks whether a recording with an abnormal label tends to receive a higher score, whereas **forecast accuracy** asks how close a predicted future was to the actual joint positions. A small, repeatable movement can be easy to predict and still carry an abnormal label.

A bootstrap interval repeatedly resamples whole recordings to see how much a comparison changes. Resampling individual frames would treat highly related observations as independent. A **seed** sets the starting point for random choices in a training run. Trying several seeds shows whether changing those choices affects the results. Each run still uses the same people. This experiment used one seed for the classifier release and three for the forecasting comparison. The test recordings came from the study's existing data collection. Wider clinical generalization would require independent participants, acquisition settings and validation. ## Check your understanding

1. **A filmmaker uploads a single walk split into three annotated sequences.** Where should they go during the classifier evaluation? **Answer:** Keep all three in the same source partition. More clips improve coverage of that recording but do not create independent test recordings.
2. **The pose detector misses a wrist and the experiment hides a visible knee.** Which can supply a training target? **Answer:** The knee. The experiment saved its observed position before hiding it. There is no measured wrist target.
3. **A staged model scores 90.9%. Why keep the mixed model in the headline comparison?** **Answer:** The researchers chose the main model before training the final versions. Choosing whichever model scored highest on 17 test videos would use the test set for model selection.
4. **Design a stronger check of movement timing.** **One answer:** Recruit multiple participants per group. Reserve complete participants before any training. Have reviewers independently mark foot contacts. Compare the timing estimates of pretrained and randomly initialized features with a simple ankle-position rule on the same held-out walks and camera views.
