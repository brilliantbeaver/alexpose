# Does the timing of the other leg help predict a step?

Pause a side-view video of someone walking. Their right ankle is behind their hip. Would knowing where the **left** ankle was over the past second improve your guess about where the right ankle will be next? If it does, how could you tell whether the timing of those earlier positions mattered, or whether the last picture already told you most of what you needed?

This is the question behind notebooks 10–16. The earlier S-JEPA experiment learned numerical representations of masked skeleton observations. The restart deliberately made the prediction task easier to inspect. It fitted small models that predict **future joint coordinates**, compared their predictions with simple alternatives, and challenged the interpretation of their errors. The distinction matters whenever you describe the results. An error reduction for a model using both legs is evidence about that coordinate predictor. It cannot by itself establish a benefit from S-JEPA pretraining. [Restart study protocol](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/restart/protocol.md)

## 10 · Count the walking observations

Open the inventory cell. It reads the transformed GAVD annotation files and keeps one record of each sequence. The original upstream CSVs preserve where the annotations came from. Counting those CSVs again would duplicate the observations. An annotated walk can produce several overlapping windows; one recording can contain several annotated walks. A sequence ID is useful for finding a walk again, but does not establish that the person appears nowhere else.

Follow a normal recording through the counting process. All 32 original source videos with a normal annotation were already in the selected scope. After quality and observation requirements, 29 recording groups provided normal examples. Adding more abnormal categories would not supply more independent examples of normal gait. Across normal, cerebral palsy, myopathic, Parkinson’s and stroke annotations, the evaluated set contained 91 recordings and 2,815 windows. These are counts at different levels. The strength of the normal forecasting test depends much more on those 29 recordings than the total window count might suggest. ## 11 · Find the right person, frame and time

The notebook selects the first eligible example in a fixed sort order, decodes a real video frame and calls the shared Ambient pose estimator. The source annotation numbers its first frame **1**. OpenCV numbers it **0**. For illustration, annotation frame 25 corresponds to decoder index 24. In an ideal constant-rate 24-frames-per-second clip starting at zero, that frame occurs at 1.000 seconds. Accidentally asking for index 25 would advance the example by another 1/24 second.

A timestamp and a frame count answer different questions. The researchers requested a grid with one observation every 0.1 seconds, selected the nearest actual frames and retained the decoded times. In the same illustrative clip, a request at 1.100 seconds falls between native frames at 1.0833 and 1.1250 seconds. The nearer is 1.0833 seconds; a stored coordinate at that frame is an observation. Inventing a landmark halfway between frames would create a different kind of measurement. In the study, the elapsed time from the final input to the final future observation ranged from 0.767 to 0.834 seconds around the nominal 0.8-second horizon.

Inspect the overlaid person and landmarks and the decisions recorded in the pose cache. A detected pelvis inside a walking person’s box can still belong to somebody else if two people overlap. The study’s person checks and confidence gates reduce this risk; they cannot identify every identity switch. ## 12 · Give the model a past without leaking the future

A window has 16 past samples and eight future samples. The target joints are the knees, ankles, heels and front-of-foot landmarks on both sides. Subtracting the pelvis midpoint expresses each landmark relative to the body. Dividing by a torso length estimated from the past gives the model comparable units for nearer and farther people. It still sees a 2D projection. Even an “own-leg” predictor shares some information from both sides because both hips define the pelvis midpoint.

The notebook displays a grid of valid observations, then deliberately changes future coordinates and future visibility flags. The features fed to the predictor must stay identical. A false visibility flag means the estimator did not produce a usable observation. A stored zero at that location does not mean that the ankle sat at the pelvis. An error can be calculated only when a future target has the required observed support.

Consider the control that disrupts the timing of the opposite leg. Applying the same permutation everywhere would just rename the columns; a regression could learn the new names. The corrected control shifts the historical opposite-leg samples by a **window-specific** offset, moves their validity flags along with them, and preserves the latest anchor. It gives a second model the same earlier values with their timing misaligned. [Notebook 12](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/12_restart_representation_and_controls.ipynb) · [Control-design correction](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/restart/protocol.md)

## 13 · Fit on normal walking and test on different recordings

First the notebook fits a teaching model on a small batch of real normal observations. The model learns **displacement** from the last observed position. Ridge regression penalizes large coefficients so one noisy input is less likely to dominate. If the learned displacement is zero, the prediction stays at the last position. This simple forecast is called *persistence*. In the optional shrinkage comparison, a multiplier between zero and one pulls the learned displacement toward persistence.

The demonstration’s error is computed on material used for fitting. For the actual experiment, every source was assigned to one of five outer folds. One outer fold at a time was set aside. Within the remaining normal training sources, three inner folds selected regularization strength; the optional shrinkage model also selected its multiplier there. A recording’s abnormal annotation or outer test error could not select these settings. The full run saved the assignments, decisions and input identities. [Notebook 13](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/13_restart_fit_and_cross_validation.ipynb) · [Selected settings by fold and leg](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/draft02/selection.csv)

**Try it.** A fitted model proposes a displacement of +0.40 torso lengths from an anchor of 0.10. What does a multiplier of 0.5 predict? The answer is 0.10 + 0.5 × 0.40 = **0.30**. A multiplier of zero predicts 0.10. Trying all multipliers on the final test recording and reporting the best would leak that recording’s future into model selection.

## 14 · Read the result with its controls

The primary forecast error averages eight future offsets on common observed targets. Lower mean squared error (MSE) is better. The table below multiplies MSE by 1,000 for readability. Every model faced the same task on held-out normal recordings. [Notebook 14](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/14_restart_results_and_uncertainty.ipynb) · [Restart forecast table](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/draft02/forecast.csv)

| Information used | MSE × 1,000 |
| --- | ---: |
| Last position copied forward | 121.262 |
| History of the target leg | 81.744 |
| History of both legs | 80.632 |
| Both legs, with timing shifted on the opposite side | 86.541 |
| Learned transition from current posture | 71.636 |

Both-leg history reduced error by (121.262 − 80.632) / 121.262 ≈ **33.5%** compared with persistence. Yet its gain over own-leg history was only 1.112 on this scale, with a paired 95% interval from −2.525 to +5.244. This interval includes zero, so the data leave open whether the second leg helped on average. Correct alignment helped relative to the fitted shifted control, while current posture alone had the lowest error of these models. The study therefore supports useful short-term prediction without establishing that detailed bilateral timing supplied the main advantage. The intervals resample recordings using saved predictions; five overlapping training folds do not constitute five independent studies. ## 15 · Decide which sentence could go in a paper

The notebook compares the protocol, the movement-science rationale and the completed artifacts. Try defending the sentence “The model measured neurological coordination.” Where is the measurement of nerves, muscle activity or verified patient status? Those data are absent. A sentence the experiment can support is “A model trained on both legs’ past positions predicted observed future positions better than repeating the last pose.” Its comparator and its measurement are explicit.

Also ask whether a forecasting result answers an anomaly question. A restricted leg might move so little that copying its last position works unusually well. Conversely, a normally moving ankle filmed at a difficult angle could generate a large error. Before treating error as a sign of abnormal gait, the study needed to compare alternative scoring rules. ## 16 · Recalculate the anomaly comparison and design a fair mask

The *excess-error* score subtracts persistence error from the bilateral model’s error. Larger values were designated more abnormal before the restart evaluation. On the 91 source recordings, raw bilateral error had pooled AUROC **0.304**, while excess error reached **0.793**. AUROC measures how often an abnormal-annotated recording ranks above a normal one, giving half credit for ties. Later, after seeing the study results, the team audited a simpler rule: score recordings higher when the observed future moves less. This **post hoc** low-movement score reached **0.806**. The paired difference, excess minus low movement, was **−0.013**, with a 95% interval **[−0.111, 0.083]**. There is an association with the annotations, but no demonstrated extra ranking benefit over the simple rule. Both scores require the observed future. [Notebook 16](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/16_iteration02_evidence_and_mask_design.ipynb) · [Anomaly table](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/draft02/anomaly.csv) · [Score audit](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/draft02/posthoc_score_audit.json)

The second exercise designs an experiment. A joint hidden behind another person is **naturally missing**. A visible joint deliberately withheld from the model is **masked**. The laboratory compares four ways to choose past observations to hide: uniform random, adjacent time blocks, connected limb groups, and a distribution across motion levels. It protects the reference joints and latest observed anchors. For one real example, there were 174 eligible tokens and a 40% budget. Since 0.40 × 174 = 69.6, every policy hid **70**. The laboratory checked all four policies on one representative window from each of 91 sources. The 364 passing checks establish that the implemented masking rules satisfied those checks. This notebook did not fit competing masked models. [Mask laboratory validation](../../../experiments/aaai-worldmodels/docs/iteration02/mask_lab_validation.json)

**Check your interpretation.** Do the 0.793 and 0.806 scores also describe the later draft03 primary test? No. Those are pooled restart scores. In the later benchmark the team declared a different forecast score and directly compared it with low movement using pairs scored within the same fold. The outcome was 0.462 versus 0.811. That design was specified for the later run, but the GAVD material had already informed the research, so its internal outcomes remained exploratory. The later notebooks also tested fitted masking policies and transfer to another dataset. Keeping the experiment, scoring rule and unit of comparison attached to each number lets you follow how a promising idea changed when it met a stronger control.
