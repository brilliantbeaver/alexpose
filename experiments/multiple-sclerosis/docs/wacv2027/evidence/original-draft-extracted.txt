                 Evaluating Predictive Skeleton Learning for
                 Video-Based Neurological Gait Analysis
Abstract
Multiple sclerosis (MS) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are neurological conditions associated with
gait abnormalities, such as variations in stride, balance, and coordination. We investigate whether
a self-supervised skeleton representation can distinguish gait videos labeled as Normal, MS, or
PD. We compiled 88 gait sequences from 41 distinct YouTube sources and extracted 33
keypoints per frame using Google’s MediaPipe BlazePose to train a Skeleton Joint Embedding
Predictive Architecture (S-JEPA) with stochastic regional masking guided by 12 clinically
selected landmarks. The frozen encoder was evaluated using five source-grouped outer folds and
compared against mean pose, keypoint-visibility statistics, an engineered-feature Random Forest,
and a majority-class baseline. S-JEPA achieved the highest average F1 score across the three
target classes (0.468 vs. 0.452 for mean pose and 0.411 for RF), although bootstrap analysis left
both advantages uncertain. Mean pose performed better under clip weighting and in four of the
five folds. On this small-scale dataset, the findings do not provide sufficient evidence that
temporal representation learning offers an advantage over simpler pose-based representations.




1. Introduction
Gait depends on the coordinated interaction between the motor, sensory, cerebellar, spinal cord,
and brainstem systems. Neurological disorders can disrupt this coordination, resulting in altered
gait parameters. Patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) demonstrate a variety of gait abnormalities
rather than a single typical pattern due to varying location and severity of lesions [1–4].
Observed changes include shorter step length, decreased knee and ankle range of motion, greater
gait variability, longer double-support time, and impaired bilateral coordination. Some of these
impairments are associated with disability levels or become more pronounced with prolonged
walking [5–10]. Parkinson’s disease (PD) causes a number of overlapping gait abnormalities,
including decreased stride length, increased variability, freezing, and gait asymmetry [11–14].

Quantitative gait assessment typically relies on specialized systems with measuring devices,
wearable sensors, or motion capture laboratories. Although these methods allow for accurate
biomechanical measurements, they are often impractical for evaluation outside of specialized
clinical settings. Smartphone and video-based methods are other existing methods currently
being investigated for gait monitoring and neurological disease assessment [15–21]. Current
body pose estimation systems, such as OpenPose, HRNet, and BlazePose, can extract joint
trajectories directly from ordinary videos [26–28]. However, these estimators are sensitive to
camera angles, occlusions, frame rates, detector reliability, and preprocessing methods [22–25].
Therefore, the model may learn recording-specific or static information rather than meaningful
gait dynamics.

Recent skeleton-based models have used graph neural networks, transformers, and
self-supervised learning to represent human motion [29–34]. Studies show that even without
class labels, useful representations can be learned by predicting hidden features from observed
states [35–42]. These methods are promising for identifying temporal relationships between
joints. However, the effectiveness of this method on small clinical datasets remains uncertain;
such datasets may contain few independent video recordings, and repeated segments may lead to
overfitting. Under such conditions, source-grouped evaluation is necessary to prevent related
recordings from appearing in both training and test sets; moreover, the model selection process
should be independent of the final evaluation [43,44].

The aim of this study was to investigate whether a self-supervised temporal representation of
skeletal points can provide a consistent classification advantage over simpler pose-based
summaries on a small, heterogeneous video dataset. We evaluate an encoder with a compact
S-JEPA architecture using data from 88 usable video clips from 41 source recordings labeled
Normal, MS, or PD. The encoder is pre-trained without class labels, frozen, and evaluated using
source-grouped cross-validation. We compare the representations generated by this encoder with
the following benchmarks: average pose, keypoint visibility statistics, random forest model
based on engineered features, and majority class baseline.

The results show that the trained representation does not have a clear overall advantage over the
average pose. Although their overall performance is similar, differences are apparent when clips
or sources are given equal weights. Therefore, these results do not prove that learning using
temporal representations improves classification performance on the current dataset.
Furthermore, the interpretation of the results is limited by factors such as varying data collection
conditions, unverified participant information in the original recordings, the shortcomings of the
random forest feature extraction tool, and the mismatch between mirroring and masking
operations during S-JEPA training. This study therefore provides an empirical evaluation of
S-JEPA against simpler baselines and identifies the controlled comparisons needed to determine
whether its learned representations capture predictive gait information beyond static pose and
recording-related characteristics.

2. Related Work
2.1 Video-Based Gait Analysis

Pose estimators like BlazePose [28] allow for the extraction of joint trajectories from ordinary
video, without markers or specialized equipment. Stenum et al. have validated such 2D
pose-based gait measures against marker-based motion capture, finding good agreement across
most spatiotemporal parameters and expanding the validation across multiple camera
perspectives and clinical populations [22], [23]. These works generally find pose estimation to be
most reliable for sagittal-plane, lower-limb kinematics and simple spatiotemporal measures, and
less reliable under conditions of occlusion, poor camera angle, or low frame rate that affect
keypoint tracking.

The general validity of this approach has since been applied directly to neurological gait
assessment. Creagh et al. have used smartphone-based data to track MS progression over time
[15], while Eguchi et al. have found that pose-based features can help distinguish PD from
spinocerebellar degeneration, a condition with overlapping symptoms [18] -- a
differential-diagnosis setting closely related to our own three-class classification problem. This
body of work demonstrates the feasibility and clinical value of video-based gait assessment, and
also raises a concern relevant to our approach: since pose estimators are sensitive to video
recording conditions as well as the subject's gait, a model trained on heterogeneous video might
learn to predict properties of the recording process rather than the subject's gait. This concern is
the motivation for the source-grouped evaluation adopted in Section 5.2.

2.2 Skeleton-Based Representation Learning

Skeleton-based methods have demonstrated that joint-position data alone is sufficient to learn
meaningful motion representations, without the need for raw video. Approaches such as ST-GCN
[29] model the skeleton as a spatiotemporal graph, capturing spatial information between joints
and temporal changes in motion, with later methods focusing on refining how information is
shared between joints [31]. Recently, transformer-based methods such as SkeletonMAE have
adapted masked-prediction pretraining (Section 2.3) to skeleton sequences directly [34].

Within gait, skeleton-based graph architectures have focused on gait recognition (identifying
individuals by their walk) rather than clinical classification. Tian et al. combined spatiotemporal
attention with graph convolution for abnormal gait recognition [49], the closest related work to
ours, though framed as abnormality detection rather than diagnosis-specific classification. This
literature demonstrates that skeleton-only representations retain sufficient information to classify
gait patterns at the individual and abnormality level, motivating our use of a similar approach for
MS and PD.

The original S-JEPA formulation selects a set of joints to mask according to highest motion
magnitude: the joints that move the most within a clip are hidden, and the model is trained to
predict them from the remaining, lower-motion joints [41], [42]. More recent work has turned
towards masking based on anatomical structure, grouping joints by body region (e.g., arm, leg,
torso) rather than selecting purely by motion [54], and anatomically-guided architectures have
been applied to skeleton-based recognition of neurological conditions [55], though not tailored to
the specific characteristics of individual conditions. These works provide context for the
landmark-selection strategy introduced in Section 4.1 and evaluated in this study. We use clinical
literature to motivate a fixed set of masked joints, but do not directly compare this choice against
motion-magnitude or alternative anatomical masks.

2.3 Predictive and Self-Supervised Representation Learning

A broader class of work has demonstrated that useful representations can be learned without
labels by predicting hidden content from visible context. Masked autoencoders (MAE)
reconstruct masked image patches from the visible region [36]. Wei et al. demonstrated that,
rather than reconstructing raw pixel values, masked content prediction in feature space yields
representations that better preserve semantic structure [38].

Most directly relevant to the present work are joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs),
which combine masked feature prediction with an additional target encoder that undergoes a
separate momentum update. I-JEPA applies this setup to images [41], while following work
extends the feature prediction to video [42]. Abdelfattah and Alahi introduced S-JEPA for
skeleton sequences. Rather than reconstructing the coordinates of hidden joints, S-JEPA predicts
learned feature representations of those joints from the visible parts of the sequence [57].
Building on this existing framework, our study evaluates S-JEPA for neurological gait
classification using video-derived skeleton sequences.

Given our relatively small dataset compared with typical self-supervised pretraining corpora, we
also consider methodological work on cross-validation with limited sample sizes. Varoquaux
showed that cross-validation can yield highly variable error estimates with small samples [43],
while Varma and Simon demonstrated that using cross-validation for both model selection and
evaluation can introduce optimistic bias [44]. These findings motivate our use of source-grouped
cross-validation with independent model selection in Section 5.2 and our cautious interpretation
of the results in Section 7.



3. Dataset and Preprocessing
We compiled 91 MP4 gait clips spanning Normal, MS, and PD. Each filename includes an
11-character YouTube video ID. A clip is kept if at least 30% of its sampled frames contain every
x-coordinate and at least eight frames survive cleaning. Each frame is centered on the hip
midpoint and divided by the shoulder-to-hip-midpoint distance. Each surviving frame was
processed with MediaPipe BlazePose [28], which outputs 33 body landmarks per frame as
normalized (x, y) image coordinates together with a visibility score for each landmark. S-JEPA
tokenizes all 33 landmarks, using their x-coordinates, y-coordinates, and visibility values. Its
training masks are sampled over anatomically connected regions, with region-selection weights
biased toward regions containing 12 clinically selected joints; this does not restrict the model's
input to those 12 joints. The mean-pose and visibility baselines also use all 33 landmarks,
whereas the Random Forest baseline uses engineered hip, knee, and ankle angle features. Three
clips failed pose extraction, leaving 88 clips from 41 sources. The metadata does not verify
participant identity or clinical status, so we treat the labels as collection labels and the source ID
as the grouping unit. Figure 1 summarizes the processing and evaluation pipeline.

4. Methodology
4.1 Stochastic Regional Masking and Sampling Bias

Rewrite: The masked-prediction objective requires choosing which tokens are hidden from the
context encoder and predicted from the visible remainder. The original S-JEPA method uses
motion-aware masking, which preferentially masks joints with greater motion [57]. Anatomically
structured masking has also been explored in other skeleton-based self-supervised methods [54].
In this study, we use stochastic regional masking with a clinically motivated sampling bias for
the gait-classification task.
A new mask is sampled for each training window. Connected body regions (head, mouth, each
arm, trunk, and each leg) are hidden over contiguous spans of the eight time blocks until
approximately 60% of the 264 tokens are masked. To reflect clinical findings of altered gait
patterns in MS and PD [1, 8, 10, 11],we multiplied the sampling weights by 1.5 for regions
containing any of 12 clinically selected landmarks: the bilateral shoulders, hips, knees, ankles,
heels, and foot indices. In a bank of 5,000 sampled masks, the observed masking frequencies
were 77–78% for shoulders and hips, 62–63% for other leg joints, 64% for elbows and wrists,
and 58% for facial landmarks. These frequencies characterize the sampled masks rather than
necessarily reflecting the frequencies encountered during training. No ablation compared this
sampler with uniform, motion-based, or alternative anatomical masking, so its effect on
classification performance remains unknown.

4.4 Representation Readout
To gauge the utility of the learned latent representations for use in clinical settings, a frozen
feature extraction approach was used. The weights of the context encoder are frozen. A linear
classifier or lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) is trained on top of these frozen
embeddings to classify subjects into healthy controls vs multiple sclerosis based strictly on
kinematic movement patterns.

5. Experimental Setup
5.1 Baseline Methods

We compare the frozen S-JEPA encoder against four baselines. Random Forest (RF) uses hip,
knee, and ankle joint angles computed from the cached landmarks: per-side means, left/right
differences, and per-side ranges. Although the underlying feature container was designed for up
to 82 engineered gait measures, only 15 columns vary across clips in this dataset; the rest are
constant and removed before fitting. A known implementation defect causes left-ankle-range to
be filled from the right ankle's statistics, so both ankle-range columns are identical across all 88
clips; this is not corrected in the results reported here.

Mean pose averages each landmark's root-centered, torso-scaled (x, y) coordinates over the full
clip, discarding frame order. Keypoint-visibility statistics instead record each landmark's mean
and standard deviation of detector visibility over the clip, using neither coordinates nor frame
order. Both use all 33 BlazePose landmarks, not the 16-landmark subset used for S-JEPA
masking (Section 4.1), and both are classified with the same logistic-regression recipe used for
the frozen S-JEPA features, fit independently on the training partition only.

The majority class predicts the most frequent label among the training clips in each fold, using
no skeleton features.

5.2 Source-Grouped Evaluation

For the k-th outer fold, the remaining sources are divided using a four-fold method. Only the first

one inner validation holdout. Since this operation proceeds from the source to the clip and
window, each clip and window receives its source partition (Table 2). Across the five outer folds,
every usable clip appears in the test set exactly once.

6.3 Evaluation Variability
S-JEPA fold macro-F1 ranges from 0.217 to 0.780, with mean 0.452 and population SD 0.189. It
exceeds RF in three folds and mean pose in only one. S-JEPA performs best in fold 2: 0.780,
compared with 0.553 for RF and 0.187 for mean pose. In fold 4, S-JEPA scores 0.217 and misses
every ‘Normal’ -labeled test clip. The pooled advantage is therefore uneven across source groups. For
this paper, a paired source bootstrap to the saved predictions was added. It was not in the original
notebook outputs. We resampled the 16 normal, 13 MS, and 12 PD sources with replacement within
label groups, using 10000 replicates and seed 20261004. Every source draw contributes a total weight
of one across its clips, including repeated draws, and all methods use the same draw. The 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles give a conditional 95% interval of [0.343,0.589] for S-JEPA. The paired difference
is 0.057[−0.121, 0.231] against RF and 0.016[−0.121, 0.231] against mean pose, and intervals include
zero, leaving the advantage uncertain.

These intervals hold the trained models and predictions fixed. They describe source resampling
variation within this collection, but exclude retraining, model selection, seed, split, and development
uncertainty. Resampling treats sources as independent units, which unverified participant overlap
could violate. The five folds also share training data, so they are not five independent experiments.
Their SD does not provide a standard error for a simple independent-fold significance test. We leave
the working null unresolved rather than claim superiority or equivalence.
7. Discussion

7.1 Interpretation of Classification Performance

The main finding is that S-JEPA did not demonstrate a consistent classification advantage over simpler
pose-based summaries on this dataset. Its pooled source-weighted macro-F1 was 0.468, compared with
0.452 for mean pose and 0.411 for the Random Forest (RF) baseline. However, the difference between
S-JEPA and mean pose was small, mean pose performed slightly better under equal clip weighting,
and S-JEPA outperformed mean pose in only one of the five outer folds. The paired source-bootstrap
intervals included zero for both comparisons, so the observed pooled differences do not establish a
reliable advantage for S-JEPA.

The source-weighted confusion matrices (Figures 4B and 4C) show that S-JEPA correctly classifies
MS-labeled clips 42.9% of the time, while 42.6% are classified as PD. It also classified 33.3% of
Normal-labeled clips as PD. These errors may reflect overlapping gait characteristics, differences in
recording conditions, or variability in the small dataset. Because the diagnostic labels were not
independently verified, the results cannot establish which factors explain these misclassifications.

These results leave open whether the learned representation captures useful temporal information
beyond average pose. The similar performance of S-JEPA and mean pose suggests that a more
complex temporal representation was not clearly necessary to obtain the observed classification
performance in this dataset. However, this comparison alone cannot determine which cues S-JEPA
uses or establish that temporal information is unhelpful. The appropriate interpretation is that this
experiment did not establish a consistent advantage for predictive skeleton learning, rather than that
such learning cannot improve gait classification.

7.2 Optimization

Although pre-training is desirable from an optimization standpoint, its value as a representation of
real-world behavior has not yet been demonstrated. The developed encoder was compared with
simpler baselines, including mean pose, which does not use temporal sequences. Additional
pre-training improved validation performance in only two trials. These results can be considered
alongside evidence from major JEPA studies [35,36].

The training diagnostics also show that lower training loss did not consistently correspond to better
validation performance. Continuation training lowered the final sampled training minibatch loss in
every fold, but validation performance improved only in folds 3 and 4. The initial stage was selected in
folds 0–2. This inconsistency means that additional optimization steps did not reliably improve
downstream classification.
Effective-rank measurements showed variation across feature dimensions, but effective rank describes
feature diversity within the measured batches, not whether the representation contains information that
distinguishes Normal, MS, and PD. Similarly, the training-only silhouette scores were close to zero
and did not establish test-set classification ability. These diagnostics therefore provide information
about optimization and representation structure, but do not demonstrate that the encoder learned
clinically meaningful features.

7.3 Temporal Effects

Positional changes within a scene can be dynamic for two reasons, making them difficult to
distinguish. They may represent internal changes, such as continuous limb movement or body rotation,
or they may reflect changes in viewpoint drift. The aforementioned signal may therefore reflect either
gait dynamics or recording effects.

“Weighting” also has practical implications. If the number of clips varies across sources, identical
predictions could lead to different performance estimates for the two systems. Therefore, the unit of
evaluation needs to be defined explicitly in small-scale gait studies. In this study, S-JEPA’s pooled
macro-F1 was higher than mean pose under source weighting, but slightly lower under clip weighting.
This difference shows that the relative comparison depends in part on how recordings are weighted.

7.4 Limitations
A major limitation is label accuracy. These labels have not been clinically validated and may include
multiple source identifiers for the same individual, meaning that clips may not represent independent
observations. S-JEPA training did not proceed entirely as planned. Although the transformation
reflected the context and swapped left and right landmark positions, the mask position remained
unchanged. In a seed-42 audit of 512 masks, 17,931 (21.0%) of the 85,409 target slots would have
their coordinates exposed if the window were reflected. Although this information remains within the
training sample and does not cross the train-test source boundary, its impact on the training process has
not been measured.

The heterogeneous recording conditions also limit interpretation because viewpoint, resolution, frame
rate, occlusion, and pose-estimation errors may affect the extracted skeletons. In addition, the
preprocessing centers each frame on the hip midpoint and scales it by shoulder-to-hip distance. This
removes absolute pelvis motion and apparent torso size, and frame-to-frame scale changes can alter
measured displacement. Consequently, the input does not preserve every physical gait quantity exactly,
and model predictions should not be interpreted as direct measurements of walking speed or stride
length.

Finally, the paired source-bootstrap intervals hold the trained models and predictions fixed. They do
not account for retraining, model-selection, seed, or split variability. The five outer folds also share
training data, so they should not be treated as five independent experiments. These factors limit the
strength and generalizability of the performance comparisons.

7.5 Future Work
Statistics and individual performance metrics serve as descriptive comparisons. Furthermore, trained
models should be compared against untrained ones using the same architecture to investigate whether
temporal training contributes beyond the architecture itself. A controlled comparison with temporally
shuffled or otherwise order-invariant inputs could further test whether temporal order contributes
information beyond individual poses. Source-based results should also be corroborated by findings
that account for temporal order and source grouping. The Random Forest feature-extraction defect
should be corrected before its performance is used as a definitive comparison with S-JEPA.

To determine whether the clinically motivated landmark selection itself contributes to performance,
future experiments should compare this mask with alternative anatomical and motion-magnitude
masks under otherwise identical training and source-grouped evaluation conditions. The
reflection-mask mismatch should also be corrected or explicitly controlled. Clinical studies require
verified labels and independent datasets. Evaluation on an independent dataset with verified diagnostic
labels and participant identities, using participant-level separation where possible, would provide a
stronger test of whether the method generalizes beyond the current video sources.

8. Conclusion
This study evaluated a pipeline for applying Skeleton Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture
(S-JEPA) to neurological gait classification using video-derived skeleton sequences. Using 88 gait
clips from 41 distinct YouTube sources, we compared S-JEPA with simpler pose-based representations
and a Random Forest baseline. When each source was given equal total weight, S-JEPA achieved an
average F1 score of 0.468, compared with 0.452 for mean pose and 0.411 for Random Forest.
However, mean pose performed better when all clips were weighted equally and outperformed S-JEPA
in four of the five evaluation folds. Bootstrap analysis did not establish a reliable advantage for
S-JEPA over either baseline.

These findings demonstrate the feasibility of evaluating predictive skeleton representations for
neurological gait classification in a small, source-grouped video dataset, but do not establish a
consistent advantage over simpler representations. The results should also be interpreted cautiously
because the diagnostic labels were not independently verified and the dataset was limited in size.
Future work should evaluate the pipeline on larger datasets with verified diagnostic labels and
participant identities, and investigate whether alternative training strategies improve classification
performance.
