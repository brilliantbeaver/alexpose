Separating Movement Prediction from Gait Anomaly Detection: A
Reference-Controlled Benchmark
AlexPose Research Team
Author names and affiliations to be confirmed before submission
Abstract
A compact model can predict future leg positions well without
providing a better measure of gait abnormality. We demon-
strate this distinction in a benchmark for movement obser-
vations in medical world models. Models learn only from
videos annotated as normal and predict eight future observa-
tions from sixteen past observations. The internal evaluation
contains 2,815 windows from 456 sequences and 91 GAVD
source videos, including 29 normal sources. A learned transi-
tion from current posture reduces normal-source forecast error
by 40.8% relative to repeating the last pose. We then compare
six training-mask policies with matched hidden-input budgets
and an unmasked prefix baseline, keeping the model and fu-
ture targets fixed. Anatomical and motion-balanced masks do
not improve on uniform masking. We separately test a locked
score from the posture-plus-velocity model that asks whether
a source is easier or harder to predict than expected from its
movement amount and missing observations. Its within-fold
AUROC is 0.462, compared with 0.811 for a low-motion base-
line that ranks smaller displacement over the observed future
as more abnormal. Frozen transfer to a separate dataset with
90 coded participants gives 0.591 and 0.762, respectively. The
contribution is a reproducible benchmark that establishes use-
ful short-horizon forecasting and makes its limits as evidence
for anomaly detection explicit.
Introduction
Walking depends on the coordinated movement of many
joints. Changes in that coordination can make movement
more variable, more restricted, or more dependent on com-
pensation. A model that learns how normal observations
evolve could help describe such changes. For a medical world
model, this would provide a testable observation component:
a way to represent movement and check whether a short fu-
ture is consistent with the observed past. Establishing this
component requires evidence about the measurements and
the prediction task before stronger claims about patient state
or intervention are possible.
Prediction offers an appealing route to gait anomaly de-
tection. A model can learn from examples annotated as nor-
mal and assign an unusualness score when a new sequence
does not follow its expectations. However, an error score can
reflect several things at once. A nearly stationary leg may
be easy to predict even when its movement is clinically re-
stricted. A fast-moving leg may be difficult to predict because
the camera or pose estimator provides poor observations. A
model may also forecast well because current posture already
constrains the short future, without using the detailed order
of earlier movement.
These possibilities motivate our central question: can a
model recognize departures from normal movement orga-
nization beyond what is explained by movement amount,
current posture, camera view, and observation quality? We
address this question with compact models and explicit con-
trols. We first compare learned forecasting with simple ref-
erences. We then test whether hiding anatomically or tem-
porally organized parts of the past improves a fixed forecast-
ing model. Finally, we evaluate an anomaly score against a
low-motion comparator and normal-only posture and quality
controls. Each question has its own endpoint because success
on one does not imply success on the others.
The study makes three contributions. First, it provides a re-
producible observation and evaluation contract that keeps re-
lated video windows together, isolates the future from model
inputs, and compares methods on identical observed targets.
Second, it measures the value of compact state descriptions
and six budget-matched masking policies and an unmasked
prefix baseline, identifying a strong current-posture refer-
ence. Third, it tests reference-aware anomaly scoring inter-
nally and in frozen transfer, making the gap between useful
prediction and incremental anomaly ranking explicit. The
contribution is a controlled benchmark and an empirical find-
ing about evaluation. Normal-only forecasting, anatomical
masking, and motion-aware masking already have substan-
tial precedents.
Movement motivation and related work
Laboratory studies provide reasons to examine how joints
relate over time. Bilateral gait timing differs in the Parkin-
son’s disease population studied by Plotnik and colleagues
(Plotnik, Giladi, and Hausdorff 2007). Studies using muscle
recordings associate altered coordination complexity with
post-stroke walking and cerebral palsy (Clark et al. 2010;
Steele, Rozumalski, and Schwartz 2015). These findings mo-
tivate tests of timing and multijoint organization. They do
not give a unique interpretation to a video prediction error.
