What the model observes
A pose is an estimated arrangement of body joints in an
image. We use Ambient’s shared RGB-image extraction in-
terface with a pinned BlazePose weight file. Sixteen positions
describe the shoulders, arms, hips, knees, ankles, heels, and
foot indices, which are landmarks near the fronts of the feet.
We subtract the midpoint between the hips, our pelvis refer-
ence, in each frame and divide by a torso scale estimated from
past observations only. The scale is the median valid distance
between the hip midpoint and shoulder midpoint in the prefix.
These coordinates describe relative two-dimensional articu-
lation in prefix-torso units. They are not physical walking
speed or measured three-dimensional joint angles. Both hips
enter the shared reference, including in an own-leg model.
Each window has sixteen past observations and eight fu-
ture queries on a nominal 10 Hz grid, with stride eight be-
tween windows. The past spans 1.5 seconds between end-
points. Primary error averages all eight future offsets from
0.1 through 0.8 seconds, rather than only the final offset. We
select native frames nearest the grid, retain actual decoded
times, and never call an interpolated joint a measured ob-
servation. GAVD frames are one-based and decoder indices
zero-based; conversion occurs once at the decoder boundary.
Confidence must be at least 0.5. At least 70% of prefix
and future joints must be observed, and both distal legs must
pass common target-support gates. The eight predicted joints
are both knees, ankles, heels, and foot indices. Predictors re-
ceive fixed future queries from the prefix. Future confidence
and visibility determine which errors can be evaluated af-
ter the future arrives. They never select predictions, masks,
anchors, or prefix normalization. Tests change every future
coordinate and validity bit while requiring predictions and
prefix preprocessing to remain unchanged. Future-relative
target coordinates and evaluation support may change.

A damped-velocity reference extrapolates half the latest
observed rate of change using actual time gaps. Learned
ridge regressions predict displacement from the same an-
chor. Ridge regularization limits coefficient magnitude in
the small training population. We compare own-leg history,
bilateral history, current bilateral posture, and a separately
fitted opposite-leg timing control. The timing control applies
a window-specific shift while retaining the anchor. It changes
alignment rather than simply renaming feature columns.
The new compact state contains current posture, a robust
recent velocity estimate, and uncertainty-related summaries
of that estimate. Velocity is the median of valid adjacent dif-
ferences over the latest five prefix intervals. Median absolute
deviation and valid-pair counts describe its variability and
support. This arm therefore adds both movement and obser-
vation information; its effect cannot be attributed to velocity
alone. A matched current-posture ridge is the mandatory sim-
ple alternative. It learns a mapping from the current joint po-
sitions to future positions, whereas persistence copies those
positions unchanged. An untrained random encoder with a
fitted coordinate head checks whether a trained nonlinear
representation is needed.
For masking, one multilayer perceptron has two hidden
layers of width 32 and a residual coordinate head, with 31,936
parameters. It receives visible prefix coordinates, visibility
indicators, latest observed anchors, and prefix times. Hidden
values are filled only after masking. The objective is future-
coordinate squared error with equal leg weighting. A learned
representation here is the numerical summary inside this
predictor. This experiment uses coordinates as targets rather
than a teacher encoder or latent-target objective.
