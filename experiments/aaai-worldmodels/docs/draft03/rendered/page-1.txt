Separating Movement Prediction from Gait Anomaly Detection: A
Reference-Controlled Benchmark
AlexPose Research Team
Author names and affiliations to be confirmed before submission
Abstract
Clinician-annotated gait video offers a practical test of
movement-observation models for medical world models. We
study GAVD, introduced as the largest publicly accessible
collection of abnormal-gait videos annotated by expert clin-
icians. Our benchmark evaluates 456 unique annotated gait
sequences, including 141 normal sequences, represented by
2,815 forecast windows from 91 original recordings. Mod-
els learn from normal walking and predict eight future ob-
servations from sixteen past observations. Related sequences
remain together throughout validation and uncertainty estima-
tion. A learned transition from current posture reduces forecast
error by 40.8% relative to repeating the last pose. A separate
post hoc analysis that weights each normal sequence equally
gives a 41.6% reduction with recording groups preserved in
resampling. We also compare six training-mask policies un-
der matched hidden-input budgets and an unmasked prefix
baseline. Anatomical and motion-balanced masks do not im-
prove on uniform masking. A separately locked posture-plus-
velocity anomaly score has recording-level AUROC 0.462,
compared with 0.811 for low realized future movement.
Frozen transfer to a separate dataset with 90 coded partici-
pants gives 0.591 and 0.762, respectively. The contribution is a
reproducible benchmark that establishes useful short-horizon
forecasting and identifies the controls needed before forecast
errors can be interpreted as evidence of gait abnormality.
Introduction
Walking depends on the coordinated movement of many
joints. Changes in that coordination can make movement
more variable, more restricted, or more dependent on com-
pensation. A model that learns how normal observations
evolve could help describe such changes. For a medical world
model, this would provide a testable observation component:
a way to represent movement and check whether a short fu-
ture is consistent with the observed past. Establishing this
component requires evidence about the measurements and
the prediction task before stronger claims about patient state
or intervention are possible.
Prediction offers an appealing route to gait anomaly de-
tection. A model can learn from examples annotated as nor-
mal and assign an unusualness score when a new sequence
does not follow its expectations. However, an error score can
reflect several things at once. A nearly stationary leg may
be easy to predict even when its movement is clinically re-
stricted. A fast-moving leg may be difficult to predict because
the camera or pose estimator provides poor observations. A
model may also forecast well because current posture already
constrains the short future, without using the detailed order
of earlier movement.
These possibilities motivate our central question: can a
model recognize departures from normal movement orga-
nization beyond what is explained by movement amount,
current posture, camera view, and observation quality? We
address this question with compact models and explicit con-
trols. We first compare learned forecasting with simple ref-
erences. We then test whether hiding anatomically or tem-
porally organized parts of the past improves a fixed forecast-
ing model. Finally, we evaluate an anomaly score against a
low-motion comparator and normal-only posture and quality
controls. Each question has its own endpoint because success
on one does not imply success on the others.
GAVD gives this question an unusually broad collection
of clinically annotated video observations. It was introduced
as the largest publicly accessible collection of abnormal-gait
videos annotated by expert clinicians, with 1,874 normal
and abnormal walking sequences (Ranjan et al. 2025). Its
sequence annotations are intended to describe uninterrupted
walking in one camera-relative direction. We retain those
published sequence identities and analyze 456 eligible se-
quences across five gait annotations. This scale supports a
heterogeneous movement benchmark, while the relationship
between repeated sequences still determines the strength of
statistical inference.
The study makes three contributions. First, it provides a re-
producible observation and evaluation contract that keeps re-
lated video windows together, isolates the future from model
inputs, and compares methods on identical observed targets.
Second, it identifies a strong current-posture forecasting ref-
erence. It also measures the value of six budget-matched
masking policies alongside an unmasked prefix baseline.
Third, it tests reference-aware anomaly scoring internally and
in frozen transfer, making the gap between useful prediction
and incremental anomaly ranking explicit. The contribution
is a controlled benchmark and an empirical finding about
evaluation. Normal-only forecasting, anatomical masking,
and motion-aware masking already have substantial prece-
dents.
