Movement motivation and related work
Laboratory studies provide reasons to examine how joints
relate over time. Bilateral gait timing differs in the Parkin-
son’s disease population studied by Plotnik and colleagues
(Plotnik, Giladi, and Hausdorff 2007). Studies using muscle
recordings associate altered coordination complexity with
post-stroke walking and cerebral palsy (Clark et al. 2010;
Steele, Rozumalski, and Schwartz 2015). These findings mo-
tivate tests of timing and multijoint organization. They do
not give a unique interpretation to a video prediction error.
Our measurements contain projected joint positions rather
than muscle activity, verified foot contacts, neural signals, or
muscle strength. We therefore test observable relationships
without inferring a neural controller or affected side.
Human-motion forecasting already uses simple references
such as repeating the latest pose, which can be strong at
short horizons (Martinez, Black, and Romero 2017). Gait-
specific self-supervised detection also predates this work
(Duan, Wan, and Zhao 2024). Our question concerns the
additional evidence needed to interpret forecasting as an
abnormality measure under heterogeneous video observa-
tions. We do not compare headline scores across studies that
use different cohorts, labels, pose formats, or supervision. A
classifier adapted with condition labels answers a different
question from a predictor fitted only on normal recordings.
Masking deliberately hides observed input during training.
It can discourage a model from relying on a nearby measure-
ment that makes a task too easy. VideoMAE uses temporal
tubes in video representation learning (Tong et al. 2022).
MAMP uses motion-informed masking for skeletal action
representations (Mao et al. 2023). S-JEPA predicts learned
numerical summaries of withheld observations rather than
their coordinates; a separate target encoder produces the sum-
maries that guide learning (Abdelfattah and Alahi 2024). A
small summary-matching loss alone cannot establish a useful
representation because an encoder could discard information.
Recent skeleton methods make the relevant precedents es-
pecially clear. The revised SLiM method uses connected
anatomical tubes, while ASMa combines complementary
motion and spatial masking (Do et al. 2026; Anand et al.
2026). Our policies adapt these ideas to sixteen intermit-
tently observed joints and a small normal reference popu-
lation. They change the input to one coordinate predictor
rather than reproduce those larger action-recognition sys-
tems. This design tests the proposed information constraints
before changing the representation objective or increasing
model size.

Internal and external populations
The complete GAVD inventory contains 1,874 annotation-
defined walking sequences. We rebuild the local inventory
from the condition folders, excluding the pinned provenance
copy from counts (Ranjan et al. 2025). Our five gait anno-
tations, normal, parkinsons, stroke, cerebral palsy, and myo-
pathic, provide 666 sequences. Available media cover 652 se-
quences, and preserving two known recording-reference ex-
clusions leaves 641 extraction candidates. Fixed observation-
quality and common-target gates retain 456 sequences, in-
cluding 141 normal sequences, which yield 2,815 forecasting
windows. Table 1 reports sequence coverage first and gives
the associated recording groups for statistical interpretation.
We process complete eligible annotation spans through ver-
ified pose caches rather than use only the beginning of each
walking sequence.
