Abstract
Gait video can describe how a person’s movement changes,
but prediction error needs a reference before it can serve as an
anomaly score. A restricted yet repeatable gait can be easier to
predict than ordinary walking. We present a source-grouped
benchmark that compares learned forecasts with persistence,
which repeats the lastobservedpose. Normal-onlymodels pre-
dict eight future samples from public gait videos. Across 2,815
windows from 91 source videos, the bilateral model reduces
normal-source forecast error by 33.5%. Its prespecified ex-
cess error above persistence ranks abnormal annotations with
AUROC 0.793, compared with 0.304 for raw forecast error.
A post hoc low-movement diagnostic reaches 0.806, showing
why the reference itself must be evaluated. Current posture
also forecasts better than bilateral history. These results pro-
vide a practical way to separate forecasting skill, movement
amount, and anomaly ranking. They support an exploratory
motor-observation benchmark, without establishing clinical
diagnosis or a learned anomaly advantage over movement
alone.
Introduction
A medical world model needs observations that describe a
patient’s state and a model of how that state changes. Gait pro-
vides one possible observation. Videos reveal relative motion
of the legs even when clinical sensors are unavailable. Before
these observations support clinical simulation, however, we
need to know what their prediction errors mean.
A common approach trains on normal movement and treats
large prediction errors as evidence of abnormality. This as-
sumes that abnormal movement is harder to predict. The
assumption can fail when a restricted or repeatable pattern
produces little change between frames. Repeating the last
pose can then obtain a small error without describing healthy
movement.
We address this problem with a reference-aware evalua-
tion. The same observed future is scored against a learned
forecast and against persistence. Their difference describes
how much the learned model helps beyond holding the last
pose fixed. We then evaluate the reference itself, the impor-
tance of movement history, and the effect of keeping source
videos separate. The contribution is a reproducible bench-
mark and an interpretable score analysis. It provides a prac-
tical test of whether a gait observation model learns infor-
mation useful for anomaly ranking beyond simple movement
differences.
Neurological motivation and related work
Walking coordinates body segments while maintaining sup-
port and advancing the body. Laboratory measurements show
structured relationships between segment angles (Borghese,
Bianchi, and Lacquaniti 1996). Bilateral timing is clinically
relevant: Plotnik and colleagues measured changes in the
coordination of left and right steps in Parkinson’s disease
(Plotnik, Giladi, and Hausdorff 2007). Studies of stroke and
cerebral palsy link altered muscle-activation patterns with
impaired walking (Clark et al. 2010; Steele, Rozumalski,
and Schwartz 2015). These observations motivate testing re-
lationships between the legs. They do not imply that every
disorder increases prediction error or that video coordinates
identify a neural mechanism. The myopathic annotation also
describes a neuromuscular category, rather than a shared
neurological cause across all included conditions.
MoCoDAD uses skeleton forecasts for anomaly detection
(Flaborea et al. 2023). FSGait combines normal-only gait
reconstruction and prediction to detect postural and tem-
poral abnormalities (Duan, Wan, and Zhao 2024). GenGait
studies normative reconstruction (Motta et al. 2026), while
S-JEPA predicts hidden skeleton representations for action
recognition (Abdelfattah and Alahi 2024). Normal-only gait
prediction is therefore established prior art. Following the im-
portance of simple motion references (Martinez, Black, and
Romero 2017), our contribution evaluates forecast skill and
anomaly scores under one timing, observation, and source-
grouping contract.
Data and observation contract
The pinned local GAVD inventory contains 1,874 se-
quences from 348 source IDs (Ranjan et al. 2025). The
five selected annotations contain 666 sequences from 103
sources. Available-media checks retain 652 sequences from
98 sources. Two documented source exclusions for iden-
tity or scene problems leave 641 extraction candidates from
96 sources. Timing, geometry, observation, and common-
target gates retain 2,815 windows from 456 sequences and
91 sources. These video groups are not verified independent
participants.
