# Clinical neuromechanics evidence for a GAVD world-model study

Prepared November 14, 2026. Purpose: support a research argument about movement mechanisms and their observability; this is not diagnostic or treatment guidance. Titles, DOIs, sample sizes, and core findings below were checked against PubMed/Europe PMC records; the Curtze 2015 and Vandekerckhove 2025 full-text discussions were also consulted. An abstract-level check is not a systematic review or a formal risk-of-bias assessment. The proposed experiments are our research inferences, not results established by these clinical papers.

## The causal chain to explain

A useful forward account is **origin → altered control and/or muscle capacity → altered interaction with gravity and the ground → motion strategy → camera projection**. These arrows summarize candidate mechanisms, not a rule that identifies a cause from one gait pattern. A visible deviation can be a direct impairment, a passive mechanical consequence, an active adaptation, or a mixture. Calling it a “compensation” requires evidence about the alternative that it helps overcome.

| Category | Origin and intermediate mechanism | Concrete walking example and limit of the explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stroke | An interrupted or ruptured cerebral blood vessel injures brain tissue; consequences depend on location and severity. Weakness and coordination/balance impairments are common ([NINDS stroke overview](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/stroke/stroke-overview)). The relevant chain can include reduced voluntary drive and reduced independence of muscle activation, alongside peripheral force capacity—not spasticity alone [S1–S3]. | If plantarflexors are not activated sufficiently at late stance, the affected limb can contribute less forward propulsion; another limb can provide more propulsion while overall speed looks comparatively preserved [S2]. A knee that bends little in swing does not prove a spastic rectus femoris: inadequate preceding push-off is an alternative [S3]. Reduced propulsion and stiff-knee appearance are related hypotheses, not interchangeable measurements. |
| Parkinson’s disease (PD) | Degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons contributes to movement dysfunction; the initiating causes are incompletely understood ([NIH/NIA overview](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/parkinsons-disease/parkinsons-disease-causes-symptoms-and-treatments)). Gait also reflects non-dopaminergic systems [P3]. An experimentally supported account concerns difficulty internally regulating movement amplitude, with partly separable rhythm and coordination functions [P1–P4]. | A person may take shorter steps and use a relatively higher cadence to achieve a given speed [P1]. An arm may swing less on one side; medication can increase its amplitude without restoring bilateral coordination [P2]. Small movement is not simply “weak muscles.” Medication state, task, and disease stage matter; a single universal PD gait template is inappropriate [P4]. |
| Myopathic gait | “Myopathic” denotes a muscle-related pattern, not one central nervous system diagnosis. Muscle disorders have inherited, inflammatory, and other causes ([NLM muscle-disorder overview](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001220.htm), [NINDS inflammatory myopathies](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/inflammatory-myopathies)). In the specific example of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), deficient dystrophin and muscle-fiber damage lead to progressive weakness ([NINDS muscular dystrophy](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/muscular-dystrophy)). | With reduced hip-extension capacity, posterior trunk lean can move the upper-body load closer to/behind the hip and reduce the external hip-flexion demand; DMD data associate hip-extension weakness with posterior trunk angle and anterior pelvic tilt [M2]. Hip-abductor weakness can accompany altered lateral strategy, but “waddle = abductor weakness” is too strong: a modern longitudinal cohort linked it to wider steps rather than lateral trunk range [M1–M2]. DMD is one studied mechanism, not a definition of every myopathic annotation. |
| Cerebral palsy (CP) | CP arises from a disturbance of the developing brain; the originating disturbance is nonprogressive, while symptoms can change ([NIH CP overview](https://health.nih.gov/health-topics-a-z/cerebral-palsy), [CDC definition](https://www.cdc.gov/cerebral-palsy/about/index.html)). Altered selective control can coexist with weakness and spasticity [C1]; muscle growth and the mechanical demands of walking also matter [C2–C4]. | In one phenotype, persistent hip/knee flexion during stance gives a crouched appearance. This changes load support and can require more knee-extensor force [C3–C4]. Crouch can reduce some other muscle demands, so it may be feasible under one impairment combination even while costly in another [C4]. The clinical CP cohort included several distributions of limb involvement [C1], so crouch must not be treated as a required signature. |

**A crucial distinction:** “abnormal coupling” in a pose representation is a statistical property of observed motion. It is not equivalent to an EMG synergy, a pathological reflex, or a neural lesion. Even EMG dimensionality is not unique proof of neural modules [G1].

## Primary evidence table

The IDs connect the synthesis above to the actual study. Cross-sectional findings establish associations; simulation studies establish conditional consequences inside their model. Neither alone proves that a proposed mechanism generated an individual web video.

| ID / primary study | Population and measurements | Supported finding | Material limits for this project |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **S1. Clark et al., 2010, _Merging of healthy motor modules predicts reduced locomotor performance and muscle coordination complexity post-stroke_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00825.2009), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20007501/) | 55 people with post-stroke hemiparesis; 20 controls. Surface EMG from eight leg muscles; nonnegative matrix factorization; walking performance. | Fewer independent EMG modules in many post-stroke participants; patterns could be represented as merged control modules. Module number related to speed modulation and step-length/propulsive asymmetry. | Observational and EMG-dependent. The finding supports a reduced-control-complexity hypothesis; a pose token is not a measured motor module. Do not assert that stroke always reduces every form of movement variability. |
| **S2. Awad, Hsiao & Binder-Macleod, 2020, _Central Drive to the Paretic Ankle Plantarflexors Affects the Relationship Between Propulsion and Walking Speed After Stroke_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/NPT.0000000000000299), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31834220/) | 40 individuals after stroke. Instrumented gait analysis, dynamometry, and supramaximal electrostimulation separated voluntary drive from force capacity. | Reduced central drive independently related to paretic propulsion deficits; strength capacity alone did not. Some faster walkers with low drive relied more on nonparetic propulsion. | Cross-sectional explanatory model, not an intervention. Speed can obscure a propulsion deficit. Keypoints alone cannot separate inaccessible strength from reduced muscle force capacity. |
| **S3. Campanini, Merlo & Damiano, 2013, _A method to differentiate the causes of stiff-knee gait in stroke patients_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2013.05.003), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23755883/) | 52 stroke participants with stiff-knee gait and 20 controls at several speeds. Kinematic ankle-acceleration proxy for push-off; preswing knee-flexion velocity. | In 44/52 stroke participants, reduced knee-flexion velocity accompanied insufficient push-off; only eight lay outside the authors' reference relationship and required an additional braking explanation. | Push-off was assessed indirectly; very slow patient walking and reference-model assumptions matter. The 85% figure belongs to this selected cohort. It is not the prevalence of a cause in all stroke gait. |
| **P1. Morris et al., 1994, _The pathogenesis of gait hypokinesia in Parkinson's disease_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/117.5.1169), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7953597/) | 34 PD participants and 34 age/height-matched controls. Footswitch analysis in 10 m walking trials; speed, cadence, and stride length manipulated. | When speed was controlled, PD participants had shorter strides and higher cadence; cadence was more readily regulated than internally increasing stride length. | A specific experimental account of hypokinesia, not every PD phenotype. Speed-matched and preferred-speed comparisons answer different questions. Pelvis-centered 2D ankle excursion is not the study's metric stride length. |
| **P2. Sterling et al., 2015, _Dopaminergic modulation of arm swing during gait among Parkinson's disease patients_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3233/JPD-140447), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25502948/) | 16 PD and 17 control participants; forearm angular velocity during walking; PD tested OFF and ON dopaminergic medication. | OFF-state slower-arm amplitude and bilateral symmetry/coordination differed from controls. Medication increased slower-arm amplitude and reduced asymmetry, without improving the cross-correlation measure of coordination. | Small sample; amplitude, symmetry, and coordination are distinct endpoints. The authors' non-dopaminergic interpretation of residual coordination deficits is a hypothesis, not transmitter identification from arm movement. |
| **P3. Bohnen et al., 2013, _Gait speed in Parkinson disease correlates with cholinergic degeneration_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0b013e3182a9f558), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24078735/) | 125 PD participants without dementia and 32 controls. Dopaminergic/cholinergic PET, cognition, and an 8.5 m OFF-state walk. | The lower-cortical-cholinergic PD subgroup walked more slowly; cholinergic measures/cognition added information beyond striatal dopaminergic innervation in this cohort. | Cross-sectional association; does not establish that cholinergic loss caused an individual's slow gait. Supports distributed-system caution, not a latent neurotransmitter label for GAVD. |
| **P4. Curtze et al., 2015, _Levodopa is a Double-Edged Sword for Balance and Gait in People with Parkinson's Disease_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.26269), [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4755510/) | 104 PD and 64 controls. Inertial measures of stance, initiation, straight walking, arm motion, and turning; OFF then ON testing. | Pace and arm amplitude responded more than some timing/stability measures; responses differed with dyskinesia. Mobility cannot be reduced to one medication-sensitive scalar. | Fixed OFF→ON order, group sex imbalance, short walkway; gait variability and step width were not assessed. Do not infer medication state from an unlabelled clip or interpret all extra movement as better control. |
| **C1. Steele, Rozumalski & Schwartz, 2015, _Muscle synergies and complexity of neuromuscular control during gait in cerebral palsy_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/dmcn.12826), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26084733/) | Retrospective clinical gait EMG from 549 people with CP and 84 unimpaired individuals, ages 3.9–70 years across the total sample. | Fewer synergies described CP muscle activity; complexity related to functional impairment, selective motor control, strength, and spasticity. | Clinical cohort and EMG factorization. Broad age and presentation ranges; these group associations do not identify the mechanism of an individual CP-labelled sequence. |
| **C2. Herskind et al., 2016, _Muscle growth is reduced in 15-month-old children with cerebral palsy_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/dmcn.12950), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26510820/) | 26 children with spastic CP (8–65 months; GMFCS I–III) and 101 typically developing children; ultrasound gastrocnemius volumes and age/bone-length comparisons. | Muscle volume increased less with age in CP, with group divergence by about 15 months; bone-length growth did not differ significantly. | Age-related observational comparison, not a longitudinal causal demonstration that reduced activation creates contracture. Supports evolving muscle morphology despite a nonprogressive originating brain disturbance. |
| **C3. Steele et al., 2010, _Muscle contributions to support and progression during single-limb stance in crouch gait_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2010.04.003), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20493489/) | Subject-specific muscle-driven simulations for ten children with mild crouch and no previous surgery; kinematics and available EMG used for comparison. | Model analysis indicated less passive skeletal support and higher sustained muscle-force requirements in crouch; contributions to progression shifted toward proximal muscles. | Estimated individual muscle forces and accelerations depend on anatomy, model, and recruitment assumptions. This is not direct force measurement, and single-limb stance/mild crouch do not cover every CP gait. |
| **C4. Steele et al., 2012, _How much muscle strength is required to walk in a crouch gait?_** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2012.07.028), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22959837/) | Simulations from six children with CP and three typically developing children; muscle groups weakened individually until tracked walking could not be reproduced. | Moderate crouch required more knee-extensor capacity; moderate crouch needed less hip-abductor capacity and mild crouch less plantarflexor capacity than unimpaired gait. | Small model sample and isolated weakness assumptions. Demonstrates a tradeoff, not proof that weakness caused the adopted gait. Do not generalize to “all weak muscles cause crouch” or “crouch always requires less strength.” |
| **M1. Stage patterns and mechanistic interpretation are not a universal myopathy signature; modern treatment/cohort conditions differ. |
| **M2. Vandekerckhove et al., 2025, _Longitudinal interaction between muscle impairments and gait pathology in growing children with Duchenne muscular dystrophy_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-025-01718-5), [Full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12984-025-01718-5) | 31 boys, ages 4.6–16.4; 152 visits in 2015–2022. Authors did not find the expected abductor-weakness/lateral-trunk-ROM relationship. | Small observational cohort; initial-value and progression-rate associations differ. No unique causal inversion from pose to a particular weak muscle. These findings particularly caution against a single textbook “waddle” explanation. |
| **G1. Kutch & Valero-Cuevas, 2012, _Challenges and new approaches to proving the existence of muscle synergies of neural origin_.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002434), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22570602/) | Cadaveric experiments and biomechanical/computational models. | Low-dimensional muscle activity can arise from biomechanical/task constraints without requiring neural commands that activate fixed muscle groups. | A counterexample to unique interpretation, not proof that neural synergies never exist. It directly limits a “low-dimensional latent = discovered neural controller” claim. |

## What ordinary 2D pose can identify

The measurement distinction below is a consequence of the observation model, not a claim that the clinical papers used ordinary video. Their additional sensors and examinations are precisely what enable their stronger conclusions.

| Evidence available | Defensible description | Additional evidence needed for stronger mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeated, visible keypoint motion within a suitable view | Projected knee-angle trajectory, relative limb timing, wrist excursion, apparent trunk lean, and unsigned motion asymmetry, with detector uncertainty. | Calibrated/validated 3D motion is needed before interpreting these as anatomical joint angles or metric spatial gait measures. Occlusion, camera movement, mirroring, and out-of-plane motion require audit. |
| Small knee flexion in swing | An observed motion pattern that is consistent with multiple candidate chains [S3]. | Ground reactions/kinetics, activation/reflex evaluation, clinical range and strength assessment to distinguish inadequate push-off, braking activity, contracture, or other contributors. |
| Small/asymmetric arm excursion | An arm-motion observation with amplitude and timing assessed separately [P2]. | Medication/task history, clinical phenotype, and suitable kinematics to connect it to the clinical PD experiments. A held object, aid, occlusion, or view can also change the observation. |
| Similar average speed or cycle duration | Similar aggregate progression/timing under the measurement used. | Bilateral kinetics and force capacity/control measures to know whether movement is achieved by the same strategy [S2]. |
| Low-dimensional pose latent | A compact representation of image-plane motion. | EMG and carefully controlled biomechanical/task alternatives to evaluate muscle coordination; neural measurements or interventions for stronger neural claims [G1]. |

Uncalibrated, single-view keypoints do not directly measure muscle force, joint torque, ground-reaction force, neural drive, spasticity, rigidity, dopamine or cholinergic status, muscle pathology, lesion site, or clinical diagnosis. Even measured joint moments would not uniquely determine individual muscle forces because multiple muscle recruitment patterns can produce a net moment. A learned world model can predict observed motion well while using correlations that do not recover these latent biological causes.

For GAVD, retain the distinction between a gait-pattern annotation and verified etiology. Do not assume that a condition name supplies medication status, affected side, onset, subtype, strength testing, EMG, ground forces, or longitudinal follow-up. This document makes no claim that those measurements are present; the local data audit must establish each field actually available. Do not turn DMD findings into a DMD diagnosis for a generic `myopathic` folder.

## Research implications and concrete tests

The following are **proposed study hypotheses**. They use clinical evidence to choose relationships worth testing, while limiting the result to the observed motion domain.

1. **Test a chain across phases, not one static symptom.** From the late-stance context, predict subsequent projected knee flexion and foot trajectory. Compare target-joint history against target history plus ankle/hip/trunk context. S3 motivates the phase ordering, but successful prediction establishes cross-joint predictive information; it does not establish measured push-off or spasticity.
2. **Separate amplitude from coordination.** Use independent endpoints for projected excursion and bilateral/arm–leg timing. P1–P2 motivate asking whether a normal-motion model explains one while missing the other. Stratify views and show speed/cycle-duration sensitivity. Do not equate an amplitude residual with PD, and do not infer medication response without paired state labels.
3. **Model tradeoffs rather than “more abnormal = worse everywhere.”** C3–C4 motivate testing whether crouch-like projected posture has predictable accompanying proximal/distal motion relationships. M2 motivates a separate trunk–pelvis–step-width analysis in view-compatible clips. These should be nonexclusive motion phenotypes with uncertainty, not four hard disease templates.
4. **Measure compensation-sensitive alternatives.** Compare clips or windows with similar gross rhythm but different asymmetry/whole-body coordination. S2 motivates the possibility that similar task output hides different strategies. Without forces, describe movement strategies and predictive context—not “restored propulsion,” “recovered strength,” or neurological recovery.
5. **Use controlled edits as model probes, not virtual patients.** Shift relative limb phase while preserving common observation support and within-limb trajectories; compare whole-body phase shifts, bounded keypoint noise, and camera/scale controls. Reject discontinuous or anatomically impossible edits. Sensitivity to an edit tests a model property; it is not a simulated stroke, dopamine change, myopathy, or intervention response.
6. **Keep evidence levels visible.** Report (a) held-out forecasting/reconstruction, (b) motion-proxy agreement with independent blinded annotations, and (c) condition-label ranking separately. None substitutes for the others. Source/video grouping, view stratification, pose-quality exclusions, and uncertainty are necessary to keep a source/view classifier from masquerading as neuromechanics.

The near-term claim should be: **a normal-motion predictor captures useful relationships among body segments, and deviations from those relationships correspond to independently described movement patterns**. A biological-cause claim would require a new or linked cohort with known participants, appropriate clinical assessment, repeated tasks/states, calibrated motion and the relevant kinetic/EMG measurements. That next study could test whether the same observed trajectory arises from different combinations of neural drive, muscle capacity, and adaptation—the ambiguity the forward causal chain makes explicit.
