# S-JEPA gait portfolio scorecard

This scorecard grades all 12 portfolio items on ONE consistent scale, so the ranking is comparable across audits and world-model retrains. Think of it as a single yardstick held up against every idea, so that a strong idea and a weak one are measured the same way. If you are new to this project, you do not need to read the 12 proposals first. This page is written to stand on its own: it explains what each score means, what the one big scientific idea behind the portfolio is, and why some claims can score high while others are held back on purpose.

## How to read this scorecard

Every item earns one number, its composite, between 1.0 and 5.0. That number is built from five smaller scores, each rated 1 to 5. Here is what each one asks, in everyday words.

**Novelty (Nov): is the question new?** Are we asking something the field has not already answered, or just repeating a known result?

**Mechanism-grounding (Mech): is it tied to real biology?** Does the idea connect to how walking actually breaks in the body (a nerve pathway, a muscle, a rhythm), or is it a loose hunch?

**Generalizability (Gen): would the answer matter beyond this one dataset?** If it only holds for our 18 source videos, it scores low. If a real outside cohort could back it up, it can score high.

**Rigor (Rig): is the test fair and hard to fool?** Are there honest controls, split-by-video checks, and pre-set pass marks, so the model cannot look smart by cheating?

**Feasibility (Feas): can it actually be done given the ambition?** A bold plan that still fits the time and compute we have scores well here.

**The weighting, in plain words.** The five scores are not equal. Generalizability and Rigor matter most, a quarter each (0.25), because a finding is only worth much if it is trustworthy and travels. Novelty and Mechanism are a fifth each (0.20). Feasibility counts least, only a tenth (0.10).

**The four bands.** A composite at or above 4.3 is a Strong main-track candidate. From 3.6 to 4.2 is Competitive with revision. From 2.8 to 3.5 is Workshop or borderline. Below 2.8 is Not yet conference-level.

**Why effort is kept separate.** Effort (weeks, retrain size, data needs) is reported on its own, outside the five scores. An ambitious idea is already hard because it is ambitious. If we also docked its score for being hard, we would punish it twice. So Feasibility asks only "is it doable as scoped", and the raw cost sits beside the score, not inside it.

**Reading the composite (worked example, item 05).** Item 05 scores Nov 3, Mech 5, Gen 5, Rig 5, Feas 5. Multiply and add: 0.20*3 + 0.20*5 + 0.25*5 + 0.25*5 + 0.10*5 = 4.60. Reading the number: 4.60 clears 4.3, so item 05 is a Strong main-track candidate.

## The one big idea: conditions differ by HOW they break walking

Start with the healthy case. When walking goes well, it has two quiet properties. It is roughly **rhythmic** (steps come at a steady beat, like a metronome), and it is roughly **left-right balanced** (your left side does about what your right side does). Hold those two ideas in your head, because each condition below breaks one of them in its own way.

**LATERALIZED (one side stops matching the other).** Imagine one leg quietly doing less work than the other, step after step. In stroke, the nerve highway from brain to body crosses over (this crossing is called decussation), so a one-sided brain injury weakens the opposite side of the body (Natali/Javed StatPearls, PMID 30571044). Hemiplegic cerebral palsy comes from a one-sided early brain injury near the fluid spaces of the brain (Volpe 2009, PMID 19081519). Early Parkinson's usually starts losing dopamine on one side of the brain first, hitting the opposite side of the body (Riederer and Sian-Hulsmann 2012, PMID 22367437). The thing a skeleton can measure here: a signed left-minus-right Symmetry Ratio (Patterson 2010, PMID 19932621). "Signed" means the number tells you which side, not just how much.

**RHYTHM (the internal metronome gets shaky).** Here both sides may match fine, but the steady beat comes and goes. Parkinson's loses the automatic timing, so the duration of each stride wobbles from step to step. The measure is stride-time coefficient of variation (CV), a percent that says how much the timing jitters. Concrete anchor: 8.8 percent in people who fall versus 4.2 percent in those who do not (Schaafsma 2003, PMID 12809998; foundational work, Hausdorff 1998, PMID 9613733). **Reading the number:** higher percent means shakier timing, so 8.8 is roughly twice as jittery as 4.2.

**SYMMETRIC (both sides weak, but still matched).** Picture someone whose legs are both tired in the same way, keeping their beat but leaning forward to cope. That is myopathy, a primary muscle disease. Its asymmetry stays low (Xiong 2023, PMID 37525241) and its cadence is kept, but the pelvis tips forward more than usual (anterior pelvic tilt 16.4 degrees versus 11.6 degrees in controls, Vandekerckhove 2022, PMID 35721358).

One honest wrinkle: Parkinson's sits in TWO regions at once, lateralized early and shaky-rhythm later. So this is not a clean, tidy split into three boxes, and we say so plainly.

Now the guardrail that matters most. This biology only DEFINES what to look for and what a wrong answer would look like. It never turns 18 source videos (with all "normal" clips coming from a single video, and every score measured on data the model already saw during training) into a claim about diagnosing real patients. The map tells us where to aim. It does not promise we have arrived.

## Why some claims can score a 5 and others are capped at 3

Here is the honest rule behind the Generalizability scores. A claim earns a **5** only when a *real, separate dataset* can back it up. In plain words, that means a group of different people, gathered by someone else, that we never trained on. This is called an **external cohort**.

Why so strict? Our own data is tiny and tangled. We have only 18 source videos, and all 12 normal clips come from a single video. On top of that, every result is transductive, which means the model already saw every clip it is scored on. So a good number on our data shows the model can *tell apart clips it has memorized*, not that it works on new people. Only an outside dataset breaks that spell.

**Two claims that can reach 5.**

- **PD variability** (Parkinson's stride-timing wobble). We can check the biomarker against PhysioNet Gait-in-PD (gaitpdb, DOI 10.13026/C24H3N). Be honest, though: that is force-plate and wearable-sensor (IMU) data, not skeletons. So it confirms the biomarker at the *label* level, across a different kind of measurement (cross-modal). It is not proof that our BlazePose skeletons transfer directly.
- **Cross-view invariance** (recognizing the same walk from different camera angles). We can test this on CASIA-B, OU-MVLP-Pose, GREW, and Gait3D, which are large, non-clinical, multi-view *pose* datasets. Same data type, real outside people.

**Two claims capped near 3.** Cerebral-palsy-specific and myopathy-specific claims cannot climb higher, because no **participant-disjoint** public skeleton dataset exists for either. "Participant-disjoint" just means the test people are entirely different humans from the training people, with zero overlap. Without such a dataset, we simply cannot show the claim holds for anyone new.

This limit is not hidden. It is stated openly on every affected item.

## Per-item summaries

**05 signed-laterality-decodability (composite 4.60, Strong main-track candidate).** This item asks a simple question about the one frozen encoder we built (fingerprint prefix d0acc262): did it, on its own, learn a signed left-minus-right axis? "Signed" just means direction matters, so a left-weak walk and a right-weak walk land on opposite ends of the same line. To call it a YES, four things must all hold. A straight-line probe (a simple linear read-out) has to recover that signed axis and beat a null that only uses raw joint coordinates by at least 0.05 R-squared (reading the number: it explains a bit more of the pattern than plain coordinates do). Its mirror image must behave close to the ideal y equals negative x flip, meaning left and right cleanly swap. And the decoded sign has to be correct on at least 75 percent of held-out source videos. A NO here is still worth a lot: this "informative null" would overturn the belief that the curriculum encoder ever organized a directional asymmetry axis at all, and because the four-lane test is built to isolate the representation, it pins the blame on what the model learned, not on a weak probe.

It earns the top marks for good reasons. The mechanism is tight, since signed laterality (the Symmetry Ratio, Patterson 2010, PMID 19932621) is the discriminative axis for one-sided conditions, so Mechanism is 5. Generalizability is 5 for a specific and honest reason: what travels beyond our 18 videos is not a clinical claim about patients, it is the measurement instrument itself. The pairing of a decodability read against a fair raw-coordinate ceiling with a mirror sign-flip test is a reusable tool that any team can point at any skeleton encoder, on any cohort, to audit whether it learned side-aware laterality. The gavd5 R-squared and slope numbers are just that tool in use. The instrument is also maximally strict, with all four margins fixed before any fitting, giving Rigor 5. Novelty sits at a solid 3, because it reads an existing encoder rather than building a new object. Because it runs zero retrain on the cached 528-token tensors, Feasibility is 5. Putting the five together, 0.20*3 + 0.20*5 + 0.25*5 + 0.25*5 + 0.10*5, gives the composite 4.60.

(Effort 3 / +0 weeks, zero-retrain, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**09 reflection-equivariant-symmetry-axis (composite 4.55, Strong main-track candidate).** This item tests a design claim, meaning a claim about how you build the model rather than what data you feed it. The idea is to build the "signed axis" (a left-minus-right measure of how one side of the body differs from the other) so that it flips sign by construction, that is, it is antisymmetric by design: swap left and right, and the number automatically flips from plus to minus. The test asks whether hard-wiring that behavior beats simply letting a standard encoder learn it on its own, by at least 0.05 held-out-source R-squared on item 05's own instrument. A YES would show that baking the symmetry into the design is worth it. A NO, which is still an informative result, would overturn the intuition that hard-wiring the symmetry is a free win at this scale. Maybe the standard encoder already learned it, or maybe the signed axis is just not the bottleneck when you only have 18 source videos.

Novelty and Mechanism both score 5, because this is an inductive-bias claim (a built-in assumption) matched exactly to the neuroanatomy of one-sided gait problems. Generalizability is 4, not 5, because the clinical side stays transductive (tested only on videos the model already saw) with no external cerebral palsy or myopathy skeleton cohort to back it up. Feasibility is 3 because it needs a full five-stage curriculum retrain (600 epochs, 11,400 updates) matched to the d0acc262 lineage, which is real work.

(Effort: 3-4 / +2-3 weeks, full curriculum retrain, reuses cached 528-token tensors (core).)

**12 cross-view-invariance (composite 4.35, Strong main-track candidate).** This item asks a simple question with a clever twist: can the model treat a change in camera angle as an ACTION it is told about, rather than just noise to average away? Here that action is called "view_delta" (the amount the viewpoint shifts), and there is one strict rule: no left-right flipping is allowed, because flipping a body would fake-swap its left and right sides. A YES result would show two things at once. First, the view-as-action model stays more stable across camera angles than the usual trick of just adding mirrored copies of clips during training ("flip augmentation"), by at least a 10 percent relative drop in how much its output drifts when the view changes. Second, a "mirror probe" would catch flip augmentation red-handed, wrongly inverting a one-sided (lateralized) biomarker. A NO overturns the claim that view-as-action beats flip augmentation for robustness. The scores follow the strengths honestly. Novelty is 5 for the fresh view-as-action framing. Mechanism is 4. Generalizability is 5, the strongest external footing in the whole set, because the core arm runs on REAL outside multi-view cohorts (CASIA-B, OU-MVLP-Pose, GREW, Gait3D) using identity-disjoint leave-one-view-out testing. Rigor is 4. Feasibility is 3, because any clinical claim would need multi-view clinical data that does not exist yet.

(Effort 3 / +unbounded weeks, view-conditioned predictor retrain, needs external cohort.)

**04 resize-timing-tax (composite 4.25, Competitive with revision).** This item asks a sharp question: when every clip is forced to exactly 64 frames, how much real timing information gets thrown away? Because a short clip is stretched and a long one is squeezed to hit that same length, the true absolute cadence (steps per minute) and walking speed simply cannot be read back out. This is not just something we failed to measure in practice; it is provably impossible by the math itself (an algebraic non-identifiability, meaning the resize erases the numbers you would need). A YES result would confirm the tax: the cadence-and-speed loss (1 minus R-squared, so higher means more information destroyed) stays at or above 0.70, and the real-time double-support duration tax stays at or above 0.50. (The tax is on the wall-clock quantities the resize erases, not on within-cycle ratios, which survive.) A NO result would be a genuine surprise: some real cadence signal survives the resize anyway, which would itself be a publishable measurement artifact worth reporting.

The scores follow from this. Novelty is 4 for the clean, crisp proof. Rigor earns a 5 because the core claim is partly analytic (proven on paper) with a tightly bounded empirical check. Generalizability is 4 since it is a lesson for ANY fixed-frame skeleton pipeline, not just ours. There is no training to run, but you do need an NPZ file plus fps regeneration to recover the true time base, so Feasibility is 4.

(Effort: 1-2 / +1 weeks, zero-retrain, needs NPZ + fps regeneration.)

**11 target-isolation-substrate (composite 4.05, Competitive with revision).** This item asks the purest world-model question: what SHOULD a gait model even try to predict? To answer it cleanly, it holds the whole substrate constant (the architecture at embed 64, depth 2, 4 heads; the mask sampler; the optimizer; the number of updates; the data and the seeds) and changes ONLY the prediction target across four matched families. The encoder itself is not frozen here, it is retrained once per family, four times in all, so that each target gets a fair, freshly trained encoder built on the identical substrate. Those families are raw coordinates, one-frame motion, the S-JEPA centered-sharpened latent (the model's own summary vector), and a normalized latent regression. Because only one thing moves, any difference in results can be blamed squarely on the target choice. A YES result is concrete: one family recovers a validated biomarker above a raw-input ceiling by at least 0.05 R-squared, with everything else identical. Reading the number: 0.05 R-squared is a small but real gain that the target alone must earn. A NO would be just as useful, an informative null. It would overturn the JEPA enthusiasm that target choice is the bottleneck at small skeleton scale, and point instead at data breadth, matching the known limits where stride-time CV is not linearly decodable and asymmetry decodes weakly (R-squared about 0.154).

The scores follow from this design. Novelty 4 and Mechanism 4 are solid. Rigor earns a 5 because this matched-substrate setup is the cleanest attribution in the whole set. Generalizability 4 because the target-design principle travels beyond this cohort. Feasibility is only 2, since the work is the heaviest here: four full matched curriculum retrains, even reusing the cached 528-token tensors.

(Effort: 3-4 / +several weeks, four full matched curriculum retrains, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**01 provenance-pathway-attribution (composite 3.95, Competitive with revision).** This is the validity foundation, the item that checks whether the whole story rests on solid ground. The model tells normal walking apart from abnormal walking astonishingly well, at about 0.96 AUC. Reading the number: 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect, so 0.96 is very strong. But this item asks a hard question. How much of that separation comes from the model reading the walking itself, and how much comes from it reading the PROCESSING PIPELINE, meaning which "door" a clip came through? Most normal clips came through an augmented path, while abnormal clips came through the canonical path, so the model might be spotting the door, not the gait. A YES here would be bad news: a Provenance-Decodability Index (a measure of how much of the score is really just an acquisition shortcut) shows a large share of the headline separability is that shortcut, and a pre-registered kill gate fires. A NO (the index at or below the gate) is the good, informative result: it removes the simplest "it is just a processing artifact" worry and lets every other item trust the embedding. Rigor and Feasibility are both 5, since this is a near-zero-retrain read on the frozen checkpoint with no external cohort. Novelty is 3 (a confound audit, not a new object) and Mechanism is 3 (about acquisition, not neuroanatomy), so it lands mid-band despite top Rigor and Feasibility.

(Effort 3 / +0 weeks, zero-retrain, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**07 group-loss-supervision-isolation (composite 3.95, Competitive with revision).** This item takes the training recipe apart and tests just one piece of it: the label-aware group loss (written L_group), a term that nudges clips from the same condition to sit near each other in feature space. The question is simple but important. Does this term teach the model real structure that carries over to brand-new videos, or does it just help the model do better on videos it already saw during training? To find out, the study runs three matched versions: the term turned ON, the term turned OFF but matched, and the term turned OFF at random. It then reads the gap between transductive and inductive scores (transductive means "tested on clips the model already saw", inductive means "tested on people and videos it never saw"), plus the per-stage V-usable information (how much label-relevant signal is present after each phase of the five-stage curriculum, Stage 0 through Stage 4, not layer by layer, since the encoder is only two layers deep).

A YES result is concrete: inductive macro-F1 goes up by at least 0.05 when the term is ON, which would show the group loss builds structure that transfers. A NO overturns that belief and says the term only helps memorize seen videos.

The scores follow from this. Novelty 4, because the transductive-versus-inductive split is a genuinely new lens. Mechanism 3 and Generalizability 4 sit in the middle. Rigor 5, thanks to three matched arms and per-stage information. Feasibility 3, because it is a fold-local finetune at gavd5 scale with no fresh pretraining. Composite 3.95.

(Effort 3 / +2-3 weeks, fold-local finetune, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**02 surprise-tomography (composite 3.90, Competitive with revision).** This item asks a sharper question than most: not just *how much* the model is surprised when part of a skeleton is hidden, but *where* that surprise lands. It takes the model's masked-prediction error and lays it out as a small picture, a 12-by-16 "surprise image" (12 maskable joints down the rows, 16 time positions across the columns). In plain words, instead of one lump-sum surprise number, you get a map showing which body parts at which moments the model found hardest to predict.

A YES result would prove two things. First, that this spatial map beats a single pooled surprise number at telling conditions apart. Second, that when you inject a known deficit, its surprise mass shows up in the matching anatomical rows, above a 0.60 threshold and gaining at least 0.05 AUC. A NO would be informative too: it would overturn the claim that surprise has any usable spatial structure beyond a single number.

The scores follow from this. Mechanism 4 because surprise localizes to the biomarker regions we expect. Generalizability 4 thanks to the gaitpdb reach. Feasibility 5 because it is a test-time read on the existing frozen checkpoint with no retrain, reusing the cached 528-token tensors. Novelty 3, since it reads that existing checkpoint rather than building a new object.

(Effort 2-3 / +2 weeks, test-time pass, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**03 inference-time-motion-energy (composite 3.90, Competitive with revision).** This item asks a simple question with no retraining at all: can the frozen encoder tell us about MOTION, meaning how the body moves over time, and not just about static pose, meaning the shape of a single frozen stance? And if it can, does a motion-based score do a better job of telling apart the "broken-rhythm" condition (Parkinson's, or PD, where the internal walking clock wobbles) from the "symmetric" one (myopathy, a muscle disease that keeps both sides even and keeps the beat) than a plain position-based score does?

A YES would show that motion-versus-position energy separates PD from myopathy under a majority rule, meaning it works on at least 7 of the 12 sources and on both PD folds, using only within-window relative regularity (how steady the movement is inside one short clip). A NO is still useful: it would overturn the belief that motion energy adds a separate, readable channel on top of static posture at this window length. The scores follow from this. Mechanism 4, because the PD-versus-myopathy contrast is defined by real biology, not guesswork. Generalizability 4, from the method plus its gaitpdb reach. Novelty 3, since it is one more readout from an already-frozen encoder. And Feasibility 5, because it does no retraining and simply reuses the cached 528-token tensors.

(Effort: 3 / +2 weeks, zero-retrain, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**08 concept-bottleneck-disentangled (composite 3.75, Competitive with revision).** This is the boldest new thing we could build: instead of one blurry summary vector, we retrain the model so it carries three separate, clearly labeled "knobs" inside it, one per gait sign. There is z_asym for left-right asymmetry, z_rhythm for stride-time variability (how much step timing wobbles), and z_posture for pelvic tilt (how the pelvis leans forward). The whole point is that you could grab one knob and turn it. A YES result, what we call steerable disentanglement, means turning one knob moves only its own sign and leaves the others alone (a steerability ratio of at least 3 with leak of at most 0.2), and it does this better than a plain-coordinate baseline. That would prove the model truly separated these signs, not just tangled them together. A NO would overturn the hope that biomarker heads plus VICReg decorrelation (a trick that pushes the knobs apart) plus biased masking are enough in a small skeleton JEPA. It honestly flags z_rhythm as the riskiest, since stride-time CV is not linearly decodable from roughly 2-second windows. The idea is fresh (Novelty a joint-highest 5, Mechanism 4, Rigor 4), but Generalizability is capped at 3 because z_posture cannot be checked against any outside cerebral palsy or myopathy skeleton cohort.

(Effort: 6-8 / included weeks, full curriculum retrain plus three auxiliary heads, reuses cached 528-token tensors (core).)

**10 prediction-error-severity (composite 3.65, Competitive with revision).** This item trains a "world model" (a model that learns what normal walking looks like and expects it to keep going that way) on NORMAL walking only. Then, when it watches a new clip, it measures how badly it was surprised, and that surprise becomes a smooth, continuous severity score. The clever part is that it never trains on any abnormal labels at all. This side-steps a nasty problem in this data called label-versus-source collinearity, where "which condition" is almost the same thing as "which video". A YES result would show two things: the prediction-error can be broken apart by mechanism (the way walking is broken), and its separate channels move in the right direction when you feed in a fake, hand-made problem. For example, a one-sided knee-flexion injection should light up the asymmetry channel, and a symmetric proximal-deficit injection should light up the posture channel, both above a bar set at 80 percent of a raw-coordinate ceiling. A NO would overturn the claim that the normal-only model learned a real, structured expectation, rather than just echoing back the coordinate that was perturbed.

The scores follow from this. Mechanism and Novelty each earn 4 because label-free severity is a genuinely fresh, well-grounded idea. Generalizability is 3 because "normal" comes from a single video (3KnFt8bH3tE), so the model is transductive to just one source, and the injection test carries an acknowledged near-tautology risk. Feasibility is 3 because it needs a from-scratch normal-only train.

(Effort 3 / +several weeks, from-scratch normal-only training, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)

**06 mask-geometry-as-object (composite 3.65, Competitive with revision).** This item makes the SHAPE of the hidden region the thing being tested. Everything else is held fixed (same encoder, same compute, same training steps, same number of masked tokens per sample), so difficulty is matched and only the geometry changes. It pre-registers three mask families, each built around a known way that walking breaks, and asks which clinical axis each one lets the model recover. Family one hides one side's lower limb so the model must infill it from the other side, forcing an ASYMMETRY axis for one-sided conditions (Symmetry Ratio, Patterson 2010, PMID 19932621); this targets the weakest-decoded scalar, asymmetry R-squared about 0.154. Family two hides a contiguous half of the gait cycle; the hypothesis is that this pushes the model to lean on RHYTHM to infill the hidden half, the timing signature tied to Parkinson's loss of automatic pacing (stride-time CV, 8.8 percent in fallers versus 4.2 percent in non-fallers, Schaafsma 2003, PMID 12809998; Hausdorff 1998, PMID 9613733). This is the family most at risk of a null, because stride-time CV is not linearly decodable from the roughly 2-second windows the model sees, so the mask may not be enough to surface it. Family three hides the hips and proximal thigh on both sides, forcing a SYMMETRIC proximal pattern, matching myopathy (anterior pelvic tilt 16.4 versus 11.6 degrees, Vandekerckhove 2022, PMID 35721358; low asymmetry, Xiong 2023, PMID 37525241).

A YES result: at matched difficulty, each family raises recoverability of its own predicted axis above both a uniform mask and a marginal-matched scramble, without making the source video easier to identify. A NO overturns the belief that mask structure, rather than data scale, token geometry, or label-aware fine-tuning, is the real lever at this scale (96 sequences, 18 sources). Mechanism 4 because every family carries a verified source-to-biomarker chain. Generalizability is capped at 3 because only the PD-rhythm family has even a cross-modal external anchor (gaitpdb); the asymmetry and symmetric-proximal families have no participant-disjoint public skeleton cohort. Novelty 4 and Rigor 4 (equal-coverage difficulty control, in-space scramble, hard provenance kill gate at 0.02, permutation sanity check, non-neural floor), held below 5 because three families multiply fold-local retrains on tiny per-condition source counts (PD 2, CP 2), which also puts Feasibility at 3.

(Effort: 4-6 / +0 weeks, fold-local Stage-0 retrain per mask family, reuses cached 528-token tensors.)
