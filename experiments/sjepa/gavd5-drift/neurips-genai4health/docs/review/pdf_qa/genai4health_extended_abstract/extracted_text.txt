                                                         Abstract
1              We argue that movement summaries supplied to future health assistants should
2              preserve what was measured, how recordings were weighted, and which uses
3              were tested. A JEPA-style gait case illustrates why. For the same 64 normal-
4              annotated validation clips, feature similarity across training stages averages to
5              0.89 with equal clip weights and 0.70 with equal video weights; one video sup-
6              plies 60 clips. Neither value establishes retained predictive ability or a change
7              in patient health. An exploratory comparison also favors simple pose summaries
8              over learned features on 20 held-out videos. These observations motivate an evi-
9              dence record accompanying movement summaries and a proposed test of its value
10             for interpretation. No generative assistant or clinical intervention was evaluated.


11   1 Position and clinical relevance
12   A health assistant could eventually combine video-derived movement summaries with other patient
13   information to support mobility review. A similarity score can compare a ﬁxed recording across two
14   model versions, rather than a person’s movement across two visits. Repeated clips can also make
15   one recording dominate an apparently broad evaluation.
16   Our position is that the measurement and evaluation conditions should remain attached to movement
17   results when they are passed downstream. Model Cards already address intended uses and evaluation
18   limits [5], and the V3 framework distinguishes stages of validation for digital measurements [4]. We
19   apply those principles to a concrete gait representation case, concentrating on recording weights and
20   the distinction between model coordinates and useful function. The encoder uses a joint-embedding
21   predictive architecture inspired by S-JEPA [1]; it does not implement a clinical agent.

22   2 Case study and observations
23   The study uses 639 quality-screened clips from 97 YouTube videos drawn from GAVD, a manually
24   curated resource for clinical gait analysis [6]. Its selected annotations cover normal gait and Parkin-
25   son’s, stroke, myopathic, and cerebral-palsy categories, without independent diagnostic veriﬁcation
26   here. All clips from a video share its role: 59 videos for training, 18 for validation, and 20 for
27   testing. The evidence comes from one split and one training initialization. Video separation does
28   not guarantee person separation across uploads.
29   A compact encoder receives 33-landmark poses resized to 64 frames and predicts hidden features
30   at selected body landmarks. Training begins with normal-annotated clips, then cumulatively adds
31   the other categories. Category labels guide that order but are not targets in the encoder loss. We
32   reanalyze retained predictions and similarity summaries, without rerunning encoder training; some
33   runtime settings were not fully recorded.

40   These are different summaries of the recordings, not competing estimates of patient stability. Equal
41   video weights are not universally preferable and do not imply equal patient weights. Moreover, either
42   average can change as model coordinates change without establishing whether useful movement
43   information was lost. Functional retention needs an actual prediction task assessed across model
44   versions.

54   3 Proposal, counterarguments, and next evaluation
55   We propose that each movement summary include its measured quantity, reference model, record-
56   ing unit and weights, observed result, and interpretation limits. For the weighting example, that
57   record would state: ﬁxed clips compared across training stages; ﬁve equally weighted videos; mean
58   cosine 0.70; predictive retention and patient mobility change untested. This specializes established
59   reporting guidance to the result being interpreted, rather than proposing a new general validation
60   framework.
61   A follow-up could compare the same assistant’s interpretations with a bare score against interpre-
62   tations with this record, using blinded review of unsupported mobility claims and recognition of
63   insufﬁcient evidence. Tests should distinguish changes in recordings from model updates and inde-
64   pendently measured movement changes. The record’s beneﬁt remains a hypothesis; documentation
65   may be ignored or add burden. Clinical usefulness requires a deﬁned workﬂow and evaluation of
66   safety and human factors [7].
67   A larger model or better evaluation could favor learned representations without eliminating the need
68   to state what a summary measures. Forecasting remains untested and would require inputs and
69   preprocessing restricted to the observed past.
70   The public-video sample, uncertain cross-upload identities, estimated pose geometry, and incom-
71   plete training records limit generalization. GAVD’s annotation repository uses the MIT License;
72   separately hosted videos have additional access and use conditions [3, 2]. Project-speciﬁc ethics
73   and data-use reviews remain unresolved, and no approval or exemption is claimed. The evidence
74   supports a focused position about interpreting movement inputs, with agent behavior and patient
75   beneﬁt left for subsequent evaluation.
