                                                         Abstract

1              Movement observations could help future health assistants describe mobility, pro-
2              vided the underlying measurements support the claims made about a person. We
3              argue that summaries supplied to these systems should retain their evaluation unit,
4              model reference, and tested purpose. A reanalysis of a JEPA-style gait model
5              provides a concrete example. For the same 64 normal-annotated validation clips,
6              feature similarity across training stages averages to 0.89 when clips receive equal
7              weight and 0.70 when videos receive equal weight; one video supplies 60 clips.
8              Neither average measures a change in patient health or preservation of predic-
9              tive ability. A separate comparison on 20 held-out videos ﬁnds that simple pose
10             summaries predict the dataset annotations correctly for more videos than learned
11             features, without establishing a general disadvantage of pretraining. Building on
12             established model-reporting and clinical-validation guidance, we propose a short
13             evidence record accompanying movement summaries and describe how to test
14             whether it improves downstream interpretation. The contribution is an empirical
15             case for more precise use of movement evidence in generative health systems; no
16             clinical agent, forecasting capability, or patient beneﬁt is demonstrated.


17   1 Position: preserve what a movement result means

18   A future health assistant might combine video-derived movement summaries with a patient’s history
19   to help a clinician review mobility. An important question arises before that integration: what does
20   each summary measure? A similarity between two model representations could describe how the
21   model changed during training, how two recordings differ, or how one person moved on different
22   visits. These comparisons need different evidence, even if their outputs are all presented as a single
23   score.
24   Our position is that movement summaries supplied to generative health systems should carry the
25   information needed to interpret them: what was measured, which observations received weight,
26   and which clinical or predictive uses were tested. This is especially important when repeated clips
27   come from a few recordings or when the representation model changes. A downstream explanation
28   could otherwise attribute a model or sampling effect to a person. We examine that risk through a
29   retrospective gait case study, without claiming to have measured errors made by an assistant.
30   The position builds on existing practice. Model Cards document intended uses, evaluation condi-
31   tions, and limitations [9]. The V3 framework for digital measurement distinguishes sensor veriﬁ-
32   cation from analytical and clinical validation [7]; DECIDE-AI addresses early clinical evaluation,
33   including safety and human factors [12]. Group-aware evaluation is also established [11]. Our
34   contribution is a worked application to movement representations: a reproducible example of how
35   weighting changes a model-similarity summary, a simpler-feature comparison that limits claims

37   sults are used downstream.
38   Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) are a useful setting for this question. They learn
39   features by predicting hidden representations, as in I-JEPA and skeletal S-JEPA [2, 1]. Our compact
40   adaptation takes estimated pose as input. It does not generate clinical text or implement an agent.
41   Its relevance to generative health AI is as a possible perception component whose outputs would
42   need to be interpreted correctly. Predicting hidden features with context from throughout a clip also
43   differs from forecasting observations that have not yet occurred.

44   2 Case study and evaluation
45   Curated videos, with recordings kept separate. We use 639 quality-screened gait clips from 97
46   YouTube videos drawn from the Gait Abnormality in Video Dataset (GAVD), a manually curated re-
47   source for clinical gait analysis [10]. The selected annotations cover normal gait, Parkinson’s, stroke,
48   myopathic gait, and cerebral palsy. They are dataset annotations, not diagnoses independently veri-
49   ﬁed in this study. Each video is an uploaded recording that can contain several clips; reliable person
50   identiﬁers are unavailable.
51   The recorded split contains 59 training, 18 validation, and 20 test videos. All clips from a video
52   share its role, with assignments retained through later data-quality exclusions. Encoder ﬁtting uses
53   training videos, and validation guides model and classiﬁer selection. The ﬁnal classiﬁer is ﬁtted on
54   all 77 training and validation videos before testing on the remaining 20. This case study reports one
55   split and one training initialization, rather than repeated independent evaluations. Appendix A gives
56   the preparation and ﬁtting details.

57   What the encoder learns. A ﬁxed pose estimator supplies 33 landmarks [8]. Prepared clips are
58   centered at the hips, scaled within each clip, and resized to 64 frames. Each joint’s mean coordinates
59   over four frames become an input token. A two-layer Transformer with 64-dimensional features and
60   a pooled-context predictor estimate hidden features supplied by a slowly updated copy of the encoder.
61   Targets are selected from the shoulders, hips, knees, ankles, heels, and foot tips.
62   The objective combines smooth-L1 feature prediction with variance and covariance penalties,
63   weighted 0.10 and 0.01, respectively [3]. The penalties discourage nearly constant or redundant
64   features; there is no separate two-view invariance term. Training starts with normal-annotated clips,
65   then adds the other categories in the order listed above while retaining earlier categories. Labels
66   determine that ordering but are not prediction targets in the encoder loss. The beneﬁt of this order
67   was not tested, and it does not represent disease progression.

68   Scope of this reanalysis. The numerical results below are recalculated from retained video-level
69   predictions and counts with mean feature similarities. The supplement reproduces those calculations
70   without videos. It does not rerun pose extraction or encoder training. Some training settings were
71   not fully recorded, and the available pose records do not establish calibrated physical coordinates.
72   We therefore use this run to examine interpretation and evaluation choices, not to benchmark the
73   JEPA family or establish a clinical biomarker.

74   3 Findings
75   3.1   The averaging rule changes which recordings a result describes

76   We compare the representation of each normal-annotated validation clip after normal-only training
77   with its representation after the ﬁnal training stage. The clip itself stays ﬁxed. Cosine similarity
78   measures how closely the directions of these two feature vectors align, ranging from −1 to 1 for
79   nonzero vectors. A larger value means closer directional agreement; it is not a percentage of retained
80   information.
81   There are 64 clips from ﬁve videos. One video supplies 60 clips and has mean similarity 0.90; the
82   other four each supply one clip, with similarities ranging from 0.54 to 0.76. Averaging over clips
83   gives 0.89. Averaging ﬁrst within each video and then equally across videos gives 0.70 (Figure 1).
84   All calculations use the same recorded similarities. Only their weights change.

85    The 60-clip video receives about 94% of the weight in the ﬁrst calculation and 20% in the second.
86    Consequently, 0.89 mainly describes that recording. Equal-video weighting answers a different
87    question by giving each upload the same contribution. Neither choice is universally correct, and
88    neither guarantees equal weighting of people without reliable identities. The lesson is to specify the
89    population a summary represents before interpreting its magnitude.
90    A further boundary remains under either weighting rule. Comparing a ﬁxed clip across model ver-
91    sions measures a change in model coordinates, not a change in mobility. Coordinates can rotate
92    while a reﬁtted predictor recovers the same movement information; an almost constant represen-
93    tation can also appear stable while being uninformative. Preservation of useful function therefore
94    needs a ﬁxed movement task evaluated across model versions. No such retention experiment is
95    available here.

96    3.2   Simple pose summaries provide an essential comparison

97    We compare three inputs to separately ﬁtted logistic-regression classiﬁers: means and variability
98    of landmark positions and frame-to-frame movement, summaries of frozen learned features, and
99    landmark-availability indicators without coordinates. All use the same 20 held-out videos. The
100   third input checks whether observation availability alone carries information about the annotation
101   labels; it does not determine what information the learned encoder uses.

107   There is also a procedural limitation. Classiﬁer selection uses averaged features for each validation
108   video, whereas testing averages the category probabilities predicted for its clips. These operations
109   need not give the same answer. In addition, there is no matched untrained-encoder comparison. The
110   results therefore support retaining a pose baseline, but do not isolate the effect of pretraining or
111   prove that the learned model relies on missing landmarks.

112   4 What should accompany a movement summary?
113   We propose a short evidence record that remains attached to a summary when it enters a generative
114   health system. Its purpose is to make the relevant measurement and evaluation conditions available
115   at the point of interpretation, alongside model-level documentation. Table 2 illustrates the proposal
116   using the weighting analysis. This is a reporting recommendation and worked example, not an
117   implemented or validated agent interface.

118   This proposal extends existing reporting guidance to the speciﬁc movement result being passed
119   downstream. A model-level description alone may not reveal that one summary used clip weights
120   and another used video weights, or that their reference encoders differ. The record should also
121   distinguish an untested use from a negative result on a tested task. In this case, clinical monitoring
122   is untested; annotation classiﬁcation was tested and yielded the limited results in Table 1.
123   A focused follow-up could compare interpretations made with a bare movement score against inter-
124   pretations supplied with the evidence record. Cases should separately vary recording composition,
125   model version, and actual movement, using independently established targets for the last compari-
126   son. Prespeciﬁed outcomes could include unsupported claims about mobility change, recognition
127   of insufﬁcient evidence, and clinician review time. The same cases and assistant should be used in
128   both conditions, with blinded assessment of the resulting interpretations. Such a study would test
129   the proposed beneﬁt; the present evidence does not show that documentation alone improves trust
130   or safety.
131   Forecasting would require its own evaluation before being listed as a supported use. Our masked-
132   feature objective can use observations before and after a hidden region. A future-prediction task
133   must restrict inputs, preparation, and baseline predictors to the observed past, with an explicit time
134   horizon. Planning an intervention would need additional evidence about actions and their conse-
135   quences. These capabilities cannot be inferred from the encoder’s training objective.

136   5 Counterarguments and remaining limits
137   A stronger model could perform better. The classiﬁer comparison is small and procedurally im-
138   perfect. It cannot establish that predictive pretraining is ineffective. GaitForeMer reports improved
139   gait-severity estimation with pretraining that combines motion forecasting and activity classiﬁca-
140   tion [4]. That task and training design differ from ours. Our position would still apply to a high-
141   performing model: its summaries would need evaluation appropriate to the proposed use, and model
142   updates would need checks on useful function.

143   Weighting should follow the use, not a universal rule. Equal-video weighting may suit an
144   upload-level evaluation, while another use may call for equal patient or episode weighting. Re-


                                                          4
145   peated clips can be useful observations and are not automatically erroneous duplicates. Their number
146   should not silently determine how much an individual recording inﬂuences a population summary.
147   The present calculation demonstrates the consequence of that choice, rather than introducing a new
148   weighting method.

149   The connection to health agents is prospective. No generative assistant, clinician collaboration,
150   or care intervention is evaluated. The empirical evidence concerns a potential movement-perception
151   component. The proposed record could itself be ignored, misinterpreted, or add burden, which is
152   why its beneﬁt needs a separate test. Clinical evaluation should address performance in the intended
153   workﬂow and human factors [12]; accurate reporting of a representation experiment cannot establish
154   those outcomes.

155   Data and measurement limits. The recordings are a selected online sample. Camera view, mobil-
156   ity aids, editing, and visibility may be associated with annotations, but their effects were not isolated.
157   People may appear in more than one upload. Pose estimates are not calibrated motion-capture mea-
158   surements, and incomplete crop metadata limits their geometric interpretation. Training and the
159   feature-similarity diagnostic also prepare missing observations differently (Appendix A); this does
160   not affect the comparison that holds recorded similarities ﬁxed. These limits preclude diagnostic or
161   population-wide claims.

162   Responsible use. GAVD’s annotation repository uses the MIT License, while the separately
163   hosted videos remain subject to their own access and use conditions [6, 5]. This distinction does
164   not establish consent or institutional authorization for every reuse. A project-speciﬁc ethics determi-
165   nation and data-use review remain unresolved; no approval or exemption is claimed. The manuscript
166   reports aggregate retrospective results and does not redistribute videos or individual pose trajectories.
167   Public release decisions require the responsible authors’ review. Diagnosis, treatment recommenda-
168   tions, and deployment are outside the evidence presented.

169   6 Conclusion
170   The most informative result in this case is that changing only the averaging rule changes a feature-
171   similarity summary from 0.89 to 0.70. Its interpretation depends on which recordings receive weight
172   and which model versions are compared, while the label-prediction check shows why simple pose
173   summaries remain necessary baselines. For future health agents, we recommend preserving these
174   distinctions in the movement evidence they receive. The next step is to test whether that information
175   reduces unsupported interpretations in a deﬁned workﬂow, alongside independent validation of the
176   movement measures themselves.

223   A Preparation and evaluation details
224   Cohort selection. The selected annotation inventory contains 666 clips from 103 videos; these
225   are subset counts, not the size of GAVD. Recorded metadata availability retains 657 clips from 100
226   videos, usable video-span checks retain 655 from 98, and pose-quality checks retain 639 from 97.
227   These describe the acquisition record, not a guarantee of present-day video availability. Video roles
228   are assigned before the latter exclusions and are not redrawn afterward. The ﬁnal split contains 377
229   training clips, 131 validation clips, and 131 test clips.

230   Pose preparation. Clips are retained when at least half of the observations across the 12 selected
231   landmarks have visibility of at least 0.45. Encoder preparation additionally requires ﬁnite coordi-
232   nates for a landmark to be marked valid. Training preparation centers coordinates on the hips, with a
233   within-clip fallback for unavailable hip positions, and scales them using shoulder and hip widths in
234   the image plane. Nonﬁnite values are set to zero before resizing to 64 frames. Finite low-visibility
235   coordinates can remain in the encoder input. A four-frame token is valid only when all its obser-
236   vations are valid. Invalid tokens are excluded from target selection and pooling, but remain able to
237   inﬂuence contextual attention because the implementation has no attention padding mask.
238   The feature-similarity diagnostic additionally ﬁlls short internal gaps of at most four frames, retain-
239   ing the observation-validity mask. Its 64-dimensional representation averages valid target-encoder
240   features over the 12 selected landmarks. The classiﬁcation input instead concatenates feature means
241   and standard deviations over all valid landmarks and over the selected subset, giving 256 values.
242   These are different summaries for different tests.

243   Encoder training. Four-frame coordinate means are projected to 64 dimensions with learned joint
244   and time positions. Two Transformer blocks use four attention heads. The predictor receives aver-
245   aged visible-valid context and a target-position embedding, then applies a two-layer multilayer per-
246   ceptron. Hidden token positions remain in the encoder layout. Only valid selected-landmark tokens
247   can be prediction targets. The minimum eligible count in a batch sets a common number of targets,
248   so the realized masked fraction can differ across clips and applies only to eligible positions.
249   The saved training history records 20 epochs at each of ﬁve cumulative stages, with validation loss
250   selecting the checkpoint at each stage. The code samples videos uniformly and then clips within
251   each video. Validation losses use changing category sets and are not comparable measures of clinical
252   progress. The objective is
                      L = LSmoothL1 feature prediction + 0.10 Lvariance + 0.01 Lcovariance .
253   The regularizers operate on projected pooled features. No condition-prediction term is enabled.
254   Some optimizer and batch settings were not completely preserved as executed conﬁguration; we
255   do not present code defaults as veriﬁed runtime settings. Selected model weights are restored be-
256   tween stages without restoring matching optimizer moments, another reason not to attribute changes
257   speciﬁcally to the category order.

258   Classiﬁer ﬁtting. Pose summaries comprise coordinate means, standard deviations, mean abso-
259   lute differences between adjacent frames, and the standard deviations of those differences for 12
260   landmarks, giving 144 values. They describe normalized coordinates rather than physical velocities.
261   Landmark-availability summaries contain the observed fraction for each of 33 landmarks and each
262   of 64 resized frames, giving 97 values.
263   A separate feature scaler and class-weighted logistic regression are ﬁtted for each input. Validation
264   macro-F1 selects regularization from C ∈ {0.1, 1, 10}, followed by reﬁtting on training and val-
265   idation videos. The selected values are 10 for pose summaries, 1 for learned features, and 1 for
266   availability. Fitting and selection use averaged features per video; test predictions average clip prob-
267   abilities within each video. This mismatch should be corrected in a newly speciﬁed comparison,
268   without treating the already inspected test set as an untouched conﬁrmatory sample.
