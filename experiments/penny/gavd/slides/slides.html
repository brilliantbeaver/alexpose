A pose-sequence JEPA scaled to the whole Gait Abnormality in Video Dataset

Part 1 · the problem

Unlabeled walking is cheap, clinical labels are precious

GAVD gives us 374 walking sequences we can turn into pose data for free. Only 68 of them carry a clinical diagnosis. That mismatch is the whole reason this project exists.

  • GAVD holds 374 sequences across 11 condition folders, 91,624 rows of tracked walking in total.
  • Every one of those 374 can be pretrained on with no labels at all, because self-supervision makes the data label itself.
  • Only 68 sequences carry a clinical diagnosis: normal 12, parkinsons 9, stroke 12, cerebral palsy 15, myopathic 20.
the one idea Pretrain on all 374 unlabeled sequences, then spend the 68 labels only at the very end on a frozen-encoder probe.
Part 1 · what really survived

From 374 sequences to 1,571 clips and 42 labeled walks

The plan wants every sequence, but real video is lossy. Dead links, failed downloads, and quality filtering shrink the pool at every stage. Here is exactly what came out the other end, and it sets the ceiling on everything that follows.

  • The 374 sequences reference only 69 unique YouTube videos, since many sequences share one video. 67 of 69 downloaded, with 2 dead or blocked links.
  • 227 sequences got a real MediaPipe skeleton through download and extraction, which windows into an unlabeled pretraining corpus of 1,571 clips of shape (32, 33, 3).
  • Of the 68 labeled sequences, only 42 survived to the labeled holdout: normal 9, parkinsons 9, stroke 8, cerebral palsy 4, myopathic 12. Those become 296 windowed clips, about 7 per sequence.
why this matters later 42 labeled sequences is a very small evaluation set. Remember this number: it is the reason the honest per-sequence result is so high in variance.
Part 1 · the second problem

Squeezing a walk into 82 numbers throws the walk away

The prior best was a Random Forest of 100 trees on 82 hand-engineered gait features: 76 percent test accuracy across 5 classes on a 70/30 split. Real signal, since chance is 20 percent. But the base is shaky.

  • Describing a whole song with 82 numbers like average loudness and tempo loses the melody, and a walking cycle is no different.
  • Every new feature is a person-week of engineering, and it bakes in the designer's guesses about which motions matter.
  • Hand features do not scale to a large, varied pool of video, which is exactly what GAVD is.
the north star Match or beat that 76 percent using a frozen encoder plus a small probe, features learned rather than hand-picked.
Part 2 · the big idea

Learn first, label last

The whole plan is two steps, and the order is what matters. Step one learns from the mountain of unlabeled video. Step two spends the tiny stack of 68 labels on a small final probe.

Six notebooks carry the idea end to end: notebooks 00 through 04 build the corpus and pretrain with no labels, and only notebook 05 freezes the encoder and fits a small 5-class classifier on the pooled features of the labeled clips (296 windows from the 42 surviving labeled sequences).

Part 2 · self-supervised learning

Self-supervised learning is fill-in-the-blank

Take something you already have, hide part of it, and train the model to predict the hidden part from the visible part. No human labels anything, because the data labels itself.

Cover one leg for half a second and the frames around it already tell you what it was doing, so the model can score its own guess for free. Do this across all 374 sequences and the model is forced to learn how bodies move.

Part 2 · what makes a JEPA special

A JEPA predicts meaning, not pixels

Predicting the hidden part exactly, every joint coordinate, wastes effort on unpredictable noise like camera shake and fingertip wobble. A JEPA makes a smarter move.

  • Embedding: a short list of numbers that summarizes something, like "person walking, left leg forward, upright posture".
  • Latent space: the space of all those summaries, the compressed meaning rather than the exact appearance.
  • A JEPA predicts a summary of the hidden part in latent space, so it can ignore unpredictable detail and focus on structure that carries meaning. This is the design of I-JEPA and V-JEPA.
the name JEPA is a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. It predicts hidden parts in latent space.
Part 2 · the architecture

A JEPA has four pieces

A context encoder reads the visible part, a slow-moving EMA target encoder makes the answer key, a predictor guesses, and a loss compares the guess to the answer in latent space.

The target encoder is a slow EMA copy of the context encoder (target = m times target + (1 minus m) times context, m near 0.996). A slow copy is a stable answer key, so the model is not chasing a moving target. The predictor is a shallow MLP, and there are no negative pairs and no decoder.

Part 2 · the collapse trap and the fix

Stopping the model from cheating

If nothing stops it, the model can cheat by outputting the same constant embedding for everything. The error is zero, the loss is happy, and the model learned nothing. This is representation collapse.

  • The cheat: every clip maps to the same vector, so the prediction is always right and always useless.
  • VICReg adds a variance term that keeps each embedding dimension spread out across a batch, so no dimension can flatten to a constant.
  • It adds a covariance term that pushes dimensions to carry different information instead of copying each other.
no tricks needed Collapse is prevented purely by the slow EMA target plus VICReg. No negative examples and no decoder.
Part 2 · the twist

Run the JEPA on skeletons, never on pixels

Most JEPAs work on image or video pixels. This one runs on pose sequences, the 33 tracked joints over time, arranged as a small graph that changes frame by frame.

  • MediaPipe BLAZEPOSE_33 finds 33 landmarks in 3D per frame, grouped into 6 semantic parts: face, left arm, right arm, torso, left leg, right leg.
  • Clips are pelvis-centered and torso-normalized, so absolute camera position and scale do not matter.
  • A clip flattens to T times 33 tokens in row-major (t, j) order, so a joint at a frame is one token. Working on joints, not pixels, keeps it light enough to run on a CPU for the toy version.
why it fits gait The pose graph exposes exactly the joints and phases clinicians care about, so the task can be aimed at real mechanisms.
Part 2 · the self-supervised task

Hide blocks, not scattered dots

Hiding random scattered joints is too easy, since a neighbor usually gives the answer away. Instead we hide spatiotemporal blocks, which forces the model to reason about coordinated motion, and coordinated motion is exactly what walking is.

Style A, limb-over-time, hides one whole limb across a window of frames. Style B, time-window, hides all 33 joints in a short window. The two styles are mixed across the batch so the model cannot lean on either shortcut.

Part 3 · the six-notebook pipeline

00 Scan, then 01 Download

The first two notebooks turn a folder of CSVs into a manifest and pull the raw video down once. Each notebook writes a small cache file the next one reads, and each runs top to bottom with zero errors on a laptop CPU.

  • 00 Scan: walk all 11 condition folders and all 374 sequence CSVs into one manifest DataFrame, one row per sequence, and visualize class balance, sequence length, and the 68-sequence labeled subset. Writes manifest.csv.
  • 01 Download: dedup the manifest to unique YouTube ids (many sequences share one video), download each once, and skip anything already cached so the run is resumable. Writes download_report.csv.
robust by design Notebook 01 mirrors the alexpose YouTube handler with an inline yt-dlp fallback, so it still runs on a fresh Colab.
Part 3 · the six-notebook pipeline

02 Extract skeletons, then 03 Build the corpus

With video in hand, the middle two notebooks turn pixels into pose and pose into a clean, windowed clip bank that the JEPA can train on.

  • 02 Extract: run MediaPipe BLAZEPOSE_33 over every downloaded sequence, quality-filter, and cache one skeletons_<condition>.npz per condition. Writes extraction_report.csv, with an inline MediaPipe fallback.
  • 03 Corpus: pelvis-center and torso-normalize every sequence, slice into overlapping fixed-length windows, and stack one big unlabeled clip bank of shape (N, T, 33, 3). Writes corpus.npz plus labeled_holdout.npz.
the fair-fight guard The labeled sequences are held out of pretraining, so the final 70/30 comparison against the baseline stays honest.
Part 3 · the six-notebook pipeline

04 Pretrain the JEPA, then 05 Probe it

The last two notebooks are where the idea pays off. Notebook 04 learns the encoder on everything with no labels. Notebook 05 freezes it and spends the 68 labels.

  • 04 Pretrain: train the context encoder, EMA target encoder, predictor, and VICReg loss on the full unlabeled corpus with block masking, watching loss terms and embedding std to confirm no collapse. Writes jepa_encoder_gavd.pt.
  • 05 Probe: freeze the encoder, embed the 296 labeled clips (from the 42 surviving sequences), train linear, MLP, and RF probes, plot the label-efficiency curve, fit the neuroscience probes, run the VICReg on/off ablation, and compare all of it to the 76 percent baseline.
the payoff The encoder is trained once with no labels and reused frozen, so a good encoder reaches useful accuracy with very few labels.
Part 4 · a scaling bug we found and fixed

Bug one: the encoder could not tell time from side

Scaling up surfaced a real root-cause bug. A transformer over flattened (t, j) tokens with no positional signal is permutation-invariant, so a mean-pooled clip embedding is just a bag of coordinates that discards frame order and left/right joint identity.

  • The tell: permuting all 1,056 tokens changed the pooled embedding by only about 2e-7, and notebook 05 gave negative R-squared for temporal scalars.
  • The fix: add a learned time embedding (T, D) plus a joint embedding (33, D), added as pos[t, j] = time[t] + joint[j], the standard I-JEPA and V-JEPA design.
  • Notebook 04 saves T and N_JOINTS in the checkpoint so notebook 05 rebuilds the encoder identically, and a stale checkpoint warns and falls back rather than crashing.
why the concept tutorials skip this The concept tutorials only teach the pieces, so they omit pos-embeds. The gavd series adds them because it actually evaluates the encoder.
Part 4 · a scaling bug we found and fixed

Bug two: the loss fell, then quietly climbed

Over about 400 real steps the total loss fell for roughly 50 steps and then climbed steadily, with prediction MSE climbing too. This was not collapse: the embedding std kept rising. Rising MSE plus rising std together is the tell.

  • Why: the variance term was applied to the EMA target and kept inflating its scale, and the L2 was on the raw un-normalized target, so the distance to cover grew mechanically as the target drifted away.
  • Fix, three parts: LayerNorm the target before the L2 (V-JEPA style), regularize the online side only, and keep VICReg light as a guard rail (var 25 to 0.5, cov 1 to 0.04, gamma 1 to 0.5).
  • Verified over 400 real steps: total loss falls about 32.0 to 5.5, MSE about 1.28 to 0.22, and the embedding std rises about 0.38 to 0.76, which is the no-collapse signal.
the lesson Normalize the target, regularize the online side only, keep VICReg light. Two forces must stay balanced or the loss drifts.

Part 5 · real GAVD results

The real run is done, and the encoder is learning gait

Notebooks 04 and 05 both re-executed top to bottom in real mode on the real GAVD corpus, seed 42, on a laptop CPU. Here is what each research question actually returned. The per-clip probe numbers carry a leakage caveat that the next slide unpacks in full.

Real results: notebook 04 total loss falls 32 to 5.5 while the embedding std rises 0.38 to 0.76, so no collapse. RQ1 per-clip probes over 20 splits read MLP 0.92, linear 0.88, RF 0.88 (macro-F1 0.91, 0.87, 0.88), which look like they clear the 0.76 baseline, but see the next slide: those clips overlap so this metric is window-leaked and is not comparable to the baseline. RQ3 the frozen latent linearly preserves step_amplitude well (R-squared 0.72) and asymmetry only faintly (0.15). RQ4 embedding std with VICReg 0.89 sits above 0.77 without it, so variance and covariance do real anti-collapse work on top of the EMA target.

Part 5 · the central honest finding

The honest gap: per-clip 0.88 versus per-sequence 0.49

This is the most important slide in the deck, not a footnote. The leaky per-clip 0.88 and the honest per-sequence 0.49 are the same encoder measured two ways, and the difference between them is window leakage. The 0.88 is not comparable to the baseline, so it is not a real win.

  • The 296 labeled clips are overlapping windows from only 42 sequences, about 7 windows per sequence. A per-clip split drops windows of the same walk into both train and test, so the probe can match a test window to a near-duplicate it already saw. That is window leakage, and it inflates the number.
  • The leaky per-clip metric, which is what notebook 05 currently prints, gives linear accuracy 0.88. It sits above 0.76, but because it is window-leaked it is not comparable to the per-sequence baseline, so it is not a win.
  • The leakage-free split groups by sequence, so no walk appears on both sides. That honest per-sequence number is 0.49 plus or minus 0.17: well above the 0.20 chance level, below the 0.76 baseline, and very high variance because only 42 sequences means about 13 per test fold.
the honest thesis A frozen pose JEPA learns a gait representation that is well above chance at the sequence level but still below the 0.76 baseline. The clip-level number is inflated by window leakage and is not comparable, so it is not a win. The clip-vs-sequence gap and the tiny 42-sequence sample are the real obstacles the next iteration must close.
Part 5 · RQ2 in one picture

Fewer labels still go a long way

RQ2 is the JEPA promise made visible. A frozen encoder that already understands walking needs only a handful of labels to reach useful accuracy, and adding more labels keeps helping.

On the real run the per-clip linear probe rises from 0.75 at a quarter of the labels through 0.82 and 0.86 to 0.88 at the full budget. Read the shape, not the heights: these are per-clip accuracies and carry the same window-leakage caveat as the previous slide, so the takeaway is that the curve rises fast then flattens, which is what a good pretrained encoder should do when labels are scarce.

Part 5 · where the errors land

Four conditions separate cleanly, one is hard

Aggregating the per-clip probe over 20 splits into a row-normalized confusion matrix shows the encoder is not guessing. Normal, parkinsons, stroke, and myopathic land on the diagonal, and the one weak class is the one with the fewest sequences.

  • Normal, parkinsons, stroke, and myopathic are cleanly separated, with 0.86 to 0.91 recall on the diagonal.
  • Cerebral palsy is the weakest at 0.78 and is most often confused with myopathic (0.19), which makes clinical sense because both alter load-bearing and can look hypotonic.
  • Cerebral palsy also has the fewest sequences, only 4, so its estimates are the least reliable. This is a per-clip confusion, so it shares the window-leakage caveat.
the reading The errors are structured and clinically sensible, not random. The hard class is the rare class, which points straight at the small-sample obstacle.
Part 6 · the neuroscience connection

Does the embedding keep the axes clinicians care about?

A frozen embedding is only clinically useful if it preserves the axes that separate conditions. RQ3 tests this by reading two linearly-decodable proxies back out of the latent space with tiny linear probes.

  • The probed proxies are asymmetry_index, the left/right leg swing ratio, and step_amplitude, the mean ankle swing range, both linearly decodable from coordinates.
  • We do not probe stride_time_cv: it is nonlinear in the coordinates, so no linear probe recovers it from any embedding. A negative R-squared there is a property of the target, not the encoder.
  • Penny's feature-to-condition mapping grades features H/M/L/NA with a documented reason, for example stroke knee_asymmetry graded H from stiff-knee gait (17 degrees or more), delivered in full by early August 2026.
a hypothesis, not a guarantee RQ3 tests whether the frozen embedding preserves asymmetry and step amplitude. It is a claim the probes check, not something assumed.
